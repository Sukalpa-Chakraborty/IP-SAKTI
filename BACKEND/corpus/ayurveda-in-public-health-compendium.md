# Ayurveda in Public Health: Compendium of Select Research Papers and Related Information

## 1. Document Overview

This compendium, published by the **Central Council for Research in Ayurvedic Sciences (CCRAS)** under the **Ministry of AYUSH**, Government of India, is a collection of select research papers, project reports, and policy documents focusing on **Ayurveda in Public Health**. It was prepared for **Ayurveda Day 2018**.

The document is divided into three sections:
- **Section I:** Healthcare Seeking Behavior in Ayurveda and Traditional Medicines.
- **Section II:** Evidences on Effectiveness of Integration of AYUSH with Conventional System of Medicines.
- **Section III:** Health Policies.

The compendium includes articles on health-seeking trends, integration of Ayurveda with biomedicine, utilization of AYUSH services, and national health policies.

## 3. Section I: Healthcare Seeking Behavior in Ayurveda and Traditional Medicine

### 3.1 Healthcare Seeking Attitude and Utilization of Traditional Medicine in India – An Overview
**Authors:** N. Srikanth, Sulochana Bhat, Arjun Singh, Renu Singh  
**Source:** World Journal of Pharmaceutical Research, 2015.

**Summary:**
- India has a unique healthcare system with multiple indigenous systems (Ayurveda, Yoga, Siddha, Unani, Homoeopathy) widely accepted alongside allopathy.
- Traditional systems are preferred for chronic diseases, have fewer side effects, are affordable, and are accessible.
- Factors influencing health-seeking behavior include socio-economic variables, sex, age, social status, type of illness, access to services, and perceived quality.
- Users are often those with chronic, non-life-threatening conditions or chronic life-threatening diseases (cancer, HIV/AIDS) seeking immune support, quality of life, or relief from side effects.
- AYUSH systems have clinical competence in respiratory disorders, cardiovascular disorders, musculoskeletal disorders, skin diseases, mental health, etc.
- Policy provisions: National Health Policy 2002, National Population Policy 2000, NRHM recommend mainstreaming AYUSH.
- Studies show 71.34% of population received Ayurvedic treatment in CCRAS mobile clinics; folk medicines used by 2.33%.
- In a Delhi study, 84% of patients reported satisfactory progress after 4+ visits; 87% expressed overall satisfaction.
- A study in 35 districts found 33% preferred ISM&H for common ailments, 18% for serious ailments; actual utilization was 14%.
- Reasons for preference: no side effects, low cost. Reasons for non-preference: slow progress, non-availability of practitioners.

### 3.2 Health Care Seeking Trends in Ayurveda Clinical Research Facilities: A CCRAS Standpoint
**Authors:** Sulochana Bhat, Narayanam Srikanth, et al.  
**Source:** Journal of Research in Ayurvedic Sciences, 2017.

**Summary:**
- Analysis of CCRAS annual reports from 1991 to 2012 covering 2,386,471 new patients and 3,890,107 follow-up cases.
- 107 diseases grouped into 26 clinical categories.
- Most common diseases: osteoarthritis (9.85%), neurological diseases (7.90%), skin diseases (7.63%), cough (5.84%), pyrexia (4.12%), hyperacidity (4.09%), rhinitis (3.91%), lower backache (3.70%), colic (3.46%), dyspnea (2.25%).
- Maximum patients were from the northern zone (35.62%).
- Female follow-up patients outnumbered males (56.64% vs 43.36%), indicating better compliance.
- Period-wise analysis showed increasing OPD attendance over time, indicating improved health-seeking behavior toward Ayurveda.

### 3.3 Integration of Ayurveda with Biomedicine: A Response Identification Cross Sectional Survey
**Authors:** Sulochana Bhat, Saketh Ram Thrigulla, et al.  
**Source:** AYUSHDHARA, 2015.

**Summary:**
- Survey of 202 biomedical doctors at Safdarjung Hospital, New Delhi.
- All respondents aware of basic principles and strengths of Ayurveda.
- 73.27% aware of Ayurveda’s strength in joint disorders, 54.46% in skin diseases, 52.97% in liver disorders.
- 55.94% believed Ayurvedic medicines may have side effects; 41.09% didn’t know.
- 35% referred patients to Ayurvedic unit; reasons: biomedical medicine didn’t help (18.32%), as add-on therapy (12.87%).
- 65% did not refer; reasons: not their system (40.59%), never felt need (23.76%), not convinced about system (22.28%).
- 49% willing to participate in collaborative research.
- Best areas for integration: preventive medicine (82.67%), skin diseases (58.42%), joint disorders (46.53%).
- Suggestions: scientific validation (95.04%), IEC strategy (57.92%), collaborative studies (49.50%).

### 3.4 Status and Role of AYUSH and Local Health Traditions Under NRHM – Report of a Study
**Authors:** Ritu Priya, Shweta A.S.  
**Source:** National Health Systems Resource Centre, 2010.

**Summary:**
- Study across 18 states.
- Stand-alone AYUSH services better utilized than co-located in most states.
- OPD attendance varied from 8 to 78 patients per facility per day.
- Co-located services well utilized where functioning for several years (Tamil Nadu, West Bengal).
- Household reporting of AYUSH use in last 3 months ranged from 20% to over 90%.
- LHT use reported by 80-100% households in 14 states.
- AYUSH and LHT used for both acute and chronic conditions; only serious emergencies excluded.
- Reasons for use: effective, cheap, easily available, no side effects.
- Quality of AYUSH services: infrastructure, human resources, drug supply, and records unsatisfactory in most states.
- Doctor-population ratio improved with co-location under NRHM.
- Cross-referrals between systems were informal and verbal.

### 3.5 Utilization of Indigenous Systems of Medicine & Homoeopathy in India
**Authors:** Padam Singh, R.J. Yadav, Arvind Pandey  
**Source:** Indian Journal of Medical Research, 2005.

**Summary:**
- Study covered 35 districts, 19 states, 33,666 households, 44,639 sick persons.
- Preference for ISM&H: 33% for common ailments, 18% for serious ailments.
- Actual utilization: 14% (7.08% Ayurveda, 6.25% Homoeopathy, 0.19% Unani, 0.66% Siddha).
- Reasons for preference: no side effects (31%), cheap (30%), effective (25%), doctor easily available (11%).
- Reasons for not preferring: slow progress (28%), practitioners not available (27%), no faith (12%), medicines not available (5%).
- 38% households visited traditional healers (40% rural, 30% urban).
- Cost of ISM&H treatment lower than allopathy (Rs. 160 vs Rs. 258 for 3 months).
- Preference increased with income and literacy.

### 3.6 Salutogenesis and Ayurveda: Indications for Public Health Management
**Authors:** Antonio Morandi, Carmen Tosto, Paolo Roberti di Sarsina, Dacia Dalla Libera  
**Source:** EPMA Journal, 2011.

**Summary:**
- Ayurveda defines health as complete physical, mental, and spiritual well-being.
- Focus on predictive, preventive, and personalized medicine.
- Emphasizes relationship-based approach, similar to quantum physics, chaos theory, and epigenetics.
- Concepts of srotas (channels of information) and tridosha (Vata, Pitta, Kapha).
- Health is swastha – stability in the true self.
- Prakriti assessment determines individual constitution and health guidelines.
- Salutogenesis (Antonovsky) shares features with Ayurveda: Generalised Resistance Resources (GRRs) and Sense of Coherence (SoC).
- Ayurveda and salutogenesis are intercultural and universally applicable.
- Public health implications: low-cost personalized lifestyle counseling, prevention of chronic diseases, healthy aging, reduced drug use.

### 3.7 What could ‘Integrative’ Medicine mean? Social Science Perspectives on Contemporary Ayurveda
**Author:** V. Sujatha  
**Source:** Journal of Ayurveda & Integrative Medicine, 2011.

**Summary:**
- Critically examines the idea of integrative medicine as a marker of pharmaceuticalization of Ayurveda.
- Three phases of Ayurveda transformation: vaidya-led (pre-1950s), government-led (post-independence), biotech-pharma-led (global health market).
- Integrative medicine is problematic when there is no organizational parity between Ayurveda and biomedicine.
- Calls for focus on Ayurveda for public health rather than global health market.
- Argues for selective assimilation under medical pluralism rather than integrative medicine.
- Highlights the lack of disciplinary boundaries in Ayurveda research and the risk of erosion of Ayurvedic knowledge.
- Suggests strengthening Ayurvedic education, protocols, and professional associations.

### 3.8 Cultural Consonance, Constructions of Science and Co-existence: A Review of the Integration of TCAM in Low- and Middle-Income Countries
**Authors:** Josyula K Lakshmi, Devaki Nambiar, et al.  
**Source:** Health Policy and Planning, 2015.

**Summary:**
- Review of integration of traditional, complementary, and alternative medicine (TCAM) in LMICs with focus on India.
- Trajectory: de facto pluralism → establishment of allopathy → (de)legitimation of TCAM → de jure pluralism.
- Hierarchy of systems: allopathy at top, certain TCAM systems next, local healing traditions last.
- Rationale for inclusion: personnel needs, patient choice, nurturing local culture.
- Integration often means incorporating TCAM practitioners into allopathic facilities to practice allopathy.
- TCAM tools increasingly emulate allopathy, sometimes inappropriately.
- Tensions: philosophical disparities, unaligned notions of evidence, ethical and operational challenges.
- Strategies for equitable medical pluralism: accreditation based on performance, multidirectional information flow, clear referral protocols.

### 3.9 Utilization of Alternative Systems of Medicine as Health Care Services in India: Evidence on AYUSH Care from NSS 2014
**Authors:** Shalini Rudra, Akashi Kalra, Abhishek Kumar, William Joe  
**Source:** PLoS ONE, 2017.

**Summary:**
- Analysis of NSS 71st round (2014) data.
- 6.9% of outpatients used AYUSH care (3.5% ISM, 3.0% Homoeopathy).
- No significant rural-urban differential.
- Highest utilization in Chhattisgarh (15.4%), Kerala (13.7%), West Bengal (11.6%).
- Public health facilities play key role in rural AYUSH provisioning.
- Use lower among middle-income households; higher among poorer and richer.
- Low-income households show greater tendency for AYUSH self-medication.
- Higher utilization for chronic diseases, skin-related and musculoskeletal ailments.
- Average OOP expenditure for AYUSH medicines: Rs 270 (rural), Rs 378 (urban); lower than allopathy.
- Private sector provides ~75% of outpatient care; public sector ~20-25%.
- For ISM care, public sector share is higher (33% rural, 26% urban) than allopathy.
- Self-medication: 50% rural, 22% urban for ISM; largely based on medical advice for homoeopathy.
- Policy implications: need for accreditation, certification, awareness, and integration.

### 3.10 Pluralism in Indian Medicine: Medical Lore as a Genre of Medical Knowledge
**Author:** V. Sujatha  
**Source:** Contributions to Indian Sociology, 2007.

**Summary:**
- Critiques dichotomous typologies in medical anthropology.
- Explores multiple genres of medical knowledge among people in central Tamil Nadu.
- Examines relationship between professional, folk, and lay practitioners.
- Shows complexity of medical knowledge and inadequacy of established dichotomies.
- Argues for studying different sources of medical knowledge within Indian medicine.

### 3.11 In the Presence of Biomedicine: Ayurveda, Medical Integration and Health Seeking in Mysore, South India
**Author:** Tapio Nisula  
**Source:** Anthropology & Medicine, 2006.

**Summary:**
- Research on medical integration and health seeking in Mysore, India (2002-2003).
- Ayurvedic medicine serves as a health reserve in urban Mysore.
- Biomedical treatment is the obvious first choice; Ayurveda used when biomedicine fails.
- Lack of experience and first-hand knowledge of Ayurveda among health seekers.
- Significance of instruments, tools, and technology in expectations of proper consultation.
- Impact of medical integration critical for modern Ayurveda to thrive.
- Ayurvedic practitioners favor institutional integration and adoption of biomedical items.

### 3.12 Evaluation Study Report on Ayurvedic Dispensaries of Delhi Government
**Source:** Planning Department (Evaluation Unit), Govt. of NCT of Delhi, 2006.

**Summary:**
- 212 patients interviewed from 21 Ayurvedic dispensaries.
- 32.7% in age group 20-40; 64.1% residing within 3 km; 32.1% housewives.
- 36.8% from families with income Rs. 3000-6000/month.
- 20.7% secondary literate; 15.5% illiterate; 15.5% graduate.
- 32.6% already aware of Ayurvedic dispensaries.
- 15% getting treatment for gastrointestinal disorders.
- 49.5% reported progress after 4th or more visit.
- 49.1% attended by doctors within 15 minutes (vs 22.2% in allopathy).
- 94.3% satisfied with location; 86.8% satisfied with prescriptions and medicines.
- 98.6% favored opening more Ayurvedic dispensaries.
- Main reasons for preference: less side effects, faith in system.

### 3.15 Role of Traditional Systems of Medicine in National Health Care Systems
**Author:** Shailaja Chandra  
**Source:** Traditional Medicine in Asia, WHO, 2001.

**Summary:**
- Discusses worldwide interest in natural products and plant-based remedies.
- Different situations in different countries: India and China have parallel traditional systems; Africa moving to legitimize traditional healers; Europe and USA have growing consumer interest.
- In India, AYUSH systems have age-old acceptance and form first line of treatment for common ailments.
- Traditional medicine has a holistic approach; allopathy has reductionistic approach.
- Policy-makers face questions on integration: how to ensure expertise, quality, safety, and regulation.
- Requirements for legitimacy: national recognition, role of home remedies, licensing, GMP, national drug lists.
- Research in traditional medicine: need for evidence of efficacy.
- Raw material and standardization: quality of medicinal plants crucial.
- Integration: Indian Government policy, mainstreaming ISM&H for population stabilization, integration into RCH programme.
- Examples of integration: co-location in allopathic hospitals (RML, Safdarjung), integrated education at BHU.
- Practical areas: Yoga, Homoeopathy, Ksharasootra, Panchakarma, folk medicine.
- Issues of integration: different concepts and approaches, need for policy, regulation, and quality assurance.

### 3.16 Management of Chikungunya through Ayurveda and Siddha – A Technical Report
**Source:** Central Council for Research in Ayurveda and Siddha, 2009.

**Summary:**
- Chikungunya epidemic in India; no specific allopathic management.
- Ayurveda and Siddha offer single drugs, compound herbal and herbo-mineral combinations, and physical measures like Dhoopana (fumigation).
- CCRAS, AYUSH departments, and institutions provided healthcare services during outbreak.
- National Seminar on “Management of Chikungunya through Ayurveda and Siddha” held in March 2007.
- Observations from medical camps and documented reports show Ayurveda and Siddha regimens have role in symptom management of post-Chikungunya sequelae like arthritis and fatigue.
- Data helps in further scientific validation.

### 3.21 Effectiveness of Ayurvedic Interventions for Ante-Natal Care (Garbhini Paricharya) at Primary Health Care Level – A Multi-Centre Operational Study
**Authors:** Sulochana Bhat, Ena Sharma, et al.  
**Source:** Journal of Research in Ayurveda and Siddha, 2015.

**Summary:**
- Study by CCRAS with technical support from ICMR in Himachal Pradesh.
- 2465 pregnant women enrolled; 1746 completed.
- Interventions: AYUSH AG Tablet, AYUSH GG Tablet, AYUSH PG Tablet, AYUSH AD candy.
- Results: Hb% increased from 9.78 ± 0.79 to 10.11 ± 0.77 (p<0.001).
- 89.12% babies had birth weight >2.5 kg.
- No maternal or infant mortality.
- Vomiting improved in 84.18%; oedema improved in 79.3%.
- 85.46% normal deliveries; 75.93% institutional deliveries.
- Conclusion: Feasible to introduce Ayurveda in mainstream antenatal care; randomized controlled studies needed.

### 3.22 Approaches for Integrating Ayurveda with Conventional System in a Multispeciality Hospital for Management of Osteoarthritis (Knee)
**Authors:** Sulochana Bhat, Vikas Gupta, et al.  
**Source:** Journal of Research in Ayurvedic Sciences, 2017.

**Summary:**
- Operational study at Safdarjung Hospital, New Delhi, in collaboration with WHO India.
- 252 patients of OA knee; 201 completed.
- Interventions: Yogaraja Guggulu, Ashwagandha, Narayan taila, diet/lifestyle.
- Results: Significant improvement in KGMC and VAS scores (p<0.05).
- Reduction in rescue medicine (NSAID) intake; 73% stopped NSAID at end.
- Referrals from orthopedics to Ayurvedic unit increased from 0% to 70%.
- 63% patients reported significant benefit; 34.2% little benefit.
- Conclusion: Ayurvedic treatment effective; continued communication and scientific approaches will usher actual integration.

### 3.23 A Cross-sectional Community-based Study on Healthcare Seeking Behavior of Elderly Women for Urogenital Symptoms
**Authors:** Sulochana Bhat, Babita Yadav, et al.  
**Source:** Journal of Research in Ayurvedic Sciences, 2017.

**Summary:**
- Survey of 824 women aged 50+ in low socioeconomic areas of Delhi.
- 58.1% (479) suffered from urogenital problems.
- 56.6% did not report to family members; 54.8% did not seek medical care.
- Main reason: inhibition (62.9% for family, 49.8% for doctor).
- Conclusion: Need for IEC and BCC strategies to improve health-seeking behavior; screening and treatment facilities should be available, affordable, and reachable.

### 3.24 The Use of Ayurvedic Medicine in the Context of Health Promotion – A Mixed Methods Case Study of an Ayurvedic Centre in Sweden
**Authors:** Maria Niemi, Göran Ståhle  
**Source:** BMC Complementary and Alternative Medicine, 2016.

**Summary:**
- Case study of an Ayurvedic centre in Sweden.
- 55 clients; 91% female; mean age 47; 64% gave specific illness as reason.
- Most common illnesses: respiratory, musculoskeletal, circulatory, tumor, cutaneous.
- Qualitative results: Ayurveda used in combination with other methods; some sought as complement to conventional medicine; some found it difficult to follow lifestyle advice.
- Positive experiences of pulse diagnostics; some reported concrete physical improvement.
- Conclusion: Further research needed on specific vs non-specific effects and pulse diagnosis.

## 4. Section III: Health Policies

### 4.1 National Health Policy 2017
**Source:** Ministry of Health & Family Welfare, Government of India, 2017.

**Summary:**
- Goal: Attainment of highest possible level of health and well-being for all at all ages through preventive and promotive health care orientation and universal access to good quality health care services without financial hardship.
- Key principles: professionalism, integrity, ethics; equity; affordability; universality; patient-centered quality; accountability; inclusive partnerships; pluralism; decentralization; dynamism.
- Objectives: progressively achieve universal health coverage; reinforce trust in public health care system; align growth of private health care sector with public health goals.
- Quantitative goals: increase life expectancy to 70 by 2025; reduce under-five mortality to 23 by 2025; reduce MMR to 100 by 2020; reduce infant mortality to 28 by 2019; reduce neo-natal mortality to 16 and still birth rate to single digit by 2025; achieve 90:90:90 for HIV/AIDS; eliminate leprosy by 2018, kala-azar by 2017, lymphatic filariasis by 2017; TB elimination by 2025; reduce blindness prevalence to 0.25/1000 by 2025; reduce premature mortality from NCDs by 25% by 2025.
- Health systems performance: increase utilization of public health facilities by 50% by 2025; antenatal care >90%; skilled birth attendance >90%; >90% full immunization by 2025; meet family planning needs >90% by 2025; 80% of hypertensive/diabetic maintain controlled status by 2025.
- Cross-sectoral goals: reduce tobacco use by 15% by 2020 and 30% by 2025; reduce stunting by 40% by 2025; access to safe water and sanitation by 2020; reduce occupational injury by half by 2020.
- Health finance: increase government health expenditure to 2.5% of GDP by 2025; increase state sector health spending to >8% of budget by 2020; decrease catastrophic health expenditure by 25% by 2025.
- Policy thrust: ensuring adequate investment, preventive and promotive health, organization of public health care delivery (primary, secondary, tertiary), national health programmes, women’s health, gender-based violence, emergency care, mainstreaming AYUSH, tertiary care, human resources for health, financing, collaboration with non-government sector, regulatory framework, vaccine safety, medical technologies, public procurement, availability of drugs and medical devices, anti-microbial resistance, health technology assessment, digital health, health surveys, health research, governance, legal framework.
- AYUSH mainstreaming: access to AYUSH remedies through co-location; Yoga in schools and workplaces; standardize and validate Ayurvedic medicines; quality control; bridge course for mid-level care providers; integration of AYUSH at knowledge systems level; Ayurgenomics; sustainable livelihood through medicinal plants; certification of prior knowledge of traditional community health care providers.
- Implementation framework and way forward.

### 4.2 Situation Analyses: Backdrop to the National Health Policy 2017
**Source:** Ministry of Health & Family Welfare, Government of India, 2017.

**Summary:**
- Achievements in MDGs: MMR declined from 556 in 1990 to 167 in 2011-13; U5MR from 126 in 1990 to 49 in 2013; underweight children reduced to 29.4% in 2013-14.
- Population stabilization: TFR decreased; 11 of 20 large states achieved TFR ≤2.1; challenge in Bihar, UP, Rajasthan, MP, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh.
- Burden of disease: communicable diseases 28%, non-communicable 60%, injuries 12%.
- Social determinants: health determined by lifestyle (50%), biological and environmental (20% each), health systems (10%).
- Inequities: rural-urban differences; marginalized communities fare poorly.
- Quality of care: gaps in antenatal check-ups, institutional delivery, sterilization deaths, safe abortion.
- Disease control programmes: polio eliminated, maternal and neonatal tetanus eliminated, leprosy reduced but stagnant, kala-azar and filariasis declining, AIDS prevalence 0.27%, TB prevalence 211/100,000, malaria declining, viral encephalitis, dengue, chikungunya rising.
- NRHM: strengthened public health systems; 900,000 ASHAs; 18,000 ambulances; 178,000 health workers; cash transfers to 10 million pregnant women.
- Urban health: NUHM sanctioned in 2013.
- Cost of care: out-of-pocket expenditure 6.9% rural, 5.5% urban; 63 million pushed to poverty annually; 18% households face catastrophic expenditure.
- Publicly financed health insurance: coverage expanded from 55 million in 2003-04 to 370 million in 2014; RSBY launched in 2008.
- Healthcare industry: valued at $40 billion, projected $280 billion by 2020; growth rate 14% projected 21%.
- Non-government sector: 70% rural and 80% urban utilize private facilities; OAEs declining, corporate hospitals rising.
- AYUSH services: National AYUSH Mission launched; need to recognize private sector and not-for-profit organizations.
- Human resource development: expansion of medical, nursing, and technical education; need for rural orientation.
- Health research: Department of Health Research established 2007; modest funding <1% of public health expenditure.
- Regulatory role: drug regulation, food safety, professional education, clinical establishments.
- Investment in health care: total health spending 4.02% of GDP; government spending 1.15% of GDP; need to increase to 2.5%.

### 4.3 National Policy on Indian Systems of Medicine & Homoeopathy 2002
**Source:** Ministry of AYUSH, Government of India, 2002.

**Summary:**
- Introduction: rich heritage of medical and health sciences; vast infrastructure under-utilized; need for phased integration.
- Policy support: Government reiterated cost-effective and efficacious nature of AYUSH; budgetary support augmented.
- Financing: ISM&H gets only 2% of total health budget; need to raise to 10% of total health plan.
- Medical education: mushroom growth of sub-standard colleges; need for regulation.
- Drug standards, regulation & enforcement: safety, efficacy, quality not assured; need for GMP adherence.
- Medicinal plants: 8000 plants with medicinal properties; 500 generally used; over-exploitation and adulteration.

## 6. RAG Q&A Extraction

### Q: What is the main focus of this compendium?
**A:** Ayurveda in public health, including healthcare seeking behavior, integration with conventional medicine, and health policies.

### Q: What are the key findings on healthcare seeking behavior?
**A:** AYUSH systems are preferred for chronic diseases, have fewer side effects, are affordable, and are accessible. Utilization varies by region and socio-economic status.

### Q: What is the role of AYUSH in NPCDCS?
**A:** Integration of AYUSH with NPCDCS includes 16 classical Ayurvedic medicines, Yoga, and lifestyle interventions. It has boosted referrals and cross-referrals.

### Q: What are the key recommendations of the National Health Policy 2017 regarding AYUSH?
**A:** Mainstreaming AYUSH through co-location, Yoga in schools and workplaces, standardization and validation of Ayurvedic medicines, bridge course for mid-level care providers, and integration at knowledge systems level.

### Q: What is the CCRAS Vision Document 2030?
**A:** A strategy for research and development in Ayurveda for 15 years, aligned with Sustainable Development Goals, focusing on drug development, validation of classical formulations, and integration into national health programs.

### Q: What is the significance of the Ayurveda in Public Health compendium?
**A:** It provides evidence for the effectiveness of Ayurveda in public health, supports policy decisions, and promotes integration of AYUSH with conventional medicine.
