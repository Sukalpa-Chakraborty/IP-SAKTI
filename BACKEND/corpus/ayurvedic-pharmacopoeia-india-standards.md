# Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India — Pharmacopoeial Standards

## 1. Document overview

The **Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India (API)** is the official compendium of quality standards for Ayurvedic medicines in India. It is the document a specification, a test report, a licence file, an export dossier or a court challenge measures a product against. Published by the Government of India under the Ministry of AYUSH (earlier editions were issued under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare) through the pharmacopoeia commission, the API is treated as a **book of standards** for the drugs and formulations it covers under the Drugs and Cosmetics framework.

| Item | Position |
|---|---|
| Title | Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India (A.P.I.) |
| **Part I** | **Single drugs** (drug substances / raw drugs) — issued in successive volumes |
| **Part II** | **Formulations** (compound medicines) — issued in successive volumes |
| Published by | Government of India, Ministry of AYUSH / pharmacopoeia commission (earlier: Ministry of Health and Family Welfare) |
| Scale | Reported by the Government of India at **645 monographs of single drugs and 203 monographs of compound formulations** (Press Information Bureau figure — check the current edition, the count rises with each volume) |
| Reported volume history (Part I) | Volume I reported as 1990 (about 80 raw drugs), with subsequent volumes through the 2000s and later — verify each volume's date and monograph count from the volume itself |
| Related official volume | **Ayurvedic Formulary of India (AFI)** — Part I first published **1978**; the formulary gives the classical formulations, the pharmacopoeia gives their standards |
| Sibling compendia | Siddha Pharmacopoeia of India; Unani Pharmacopoeia; Indian Pharmacopoeia; Homeopathic Pharmacopoeia |

**Why it matters for Ayurveda:** a claim of "standardised", "heavy-metal free", "quality tested" or "pharmacopoeial grade" is only as good as the monograph behind it. The API is also the practical bridge between a classical formula (AFI / First Schedule) and a regulatory specification that a licensing authority, an exporter or a buyer will accept.

## 2. The structure of an API monograph

### Part I (single drug) monographs — typical headings

| Heading | What it fixes |
|---|---|
| **Synonyms** | Sanskrit, vernacular/regional and English names — the cross-reference layer that makes a monograph findable |
| **Source / botanical (or other) source** | The organism or material the drug is derived from, usually with family and, for herbal drugs, the plant part used |
| **Definition** | What material qualifies as *this* drug (part used, stage of harvest, processing) |
| **Characters** | Macroscopic (and where prescribed, microscopic) description — the first identity screen |
| **Identification tests** | Chemical/microscopic/thin-layer or other tests that distinguish the drug from substitutes and adulterants |
| **Tests for purity** | The quantitative purity set — e.g. foreign matter, moisture, total ash, acid-insoluble ash, extractive values and any drug-specific chemical tests |
| **Assay / standardisation** | Where prescribed, the marker or group of markers and the method against which content is judged |
| **Storage** | Conditions and, where applicable, re-test or shelf-life guidance |
| **Additional requirements where prescribed** | Limits for contaminants (including heavy metals and microbial/pesticide limits where the monograph or the applicable rules provide them) |

### Part II (formulation) monographs — typical headings

| Heading | What it fixes |
|---|---|
| **Composition** | Ingredients and their proportions/quantities as per the classical source, with the part and grade of each |
| **Direction / therapeutic class** | How the formulation is presented |
| **Description** | Physical form of the finished product |
| **Identification** | Tests for the constituent drugs in the finished product |
| **Purity and contaminant limits** | Limits applying to the finished product (including limits the monograph prescribes for heavy metals and other contaminants) |
| **Assay** | Where prescribed for the finished product |
| **Storage** | Conditions and shelf life |

**How to use a monograph:** the specification sheet for a product should map *every* test in the applicable monograph — identity, purity, assay, storage — and state the method, the acceptance criterion and the evidence. A specification that drops tests the monograph prescribes is not a pharmacopoeial specification.

## 3. What the API does and does not decide

| Question | Decided by the API? |
|---|---|
| What the identity and purity of a drug substance are | **Yes** |
| What tests and limits apply to a monographed formulation | **Yes**, as far as the monograph goes |
| Whether the product may be sold (licence) | No — the licence comes from the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules (Form 24-D route, Schedule T) |
| Whether a disease claim may be made | No — that is the advertising/claims regime |
| Whether a classical formula may be used | No — the First Schedule authoritative books and the AFI |
| Whether the sourcing is lawful | No — the Biological Diversity Act/Rules |
| Whether a patent may be granted | No — the Patents Act; the API and AFI are themselves prior art |
| Whether an export market accepts the product | No — the importing country's rules, though API/WHO methods are commonly cited in dossiers |

## 4. Interfaces with the rest of the corpus

| Regime | How the API connects |
|---|---|
| **First Schedule, Drugs and Cosmetics Act** | The Schedule fixes *which books* define classical formulas; the API fixes the *quality standard* for the drug substance and formulation. A product can be classical yet fail the API |
| **Ayurvedic Formulary of India** | AFI gives the formula and direction; API gives the tests. Both are cited together in a specification |
| **Schedule T (Good Manufacturing Practice)** | GMP is the process control; the API is the product specification. Both are needed for a compliant licence |
| **FSS (Ayurveda Aahar) Regulations, 2022** | The Regulations expressly allow an FBO to adopt purity criteria as generally accepted by pharmacopoeias — naming the **Indian Pharmacopoeia and the Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India** among them — and require the FBO to inform the licensing authority of the criteria adopted |
| **New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019** | Where new claims are made, pharmacopoeial identity does not replace clinical evidence |
| **Exports** | API test methods are commonly accepted as a basis for the specification attached to an export dossier; buyers may additionally ask for **WHO monographs/benchmarks**, European Pharmacopoeia herbal monographs or their own in-house methods |
| **Patents** | A published monograph destroys novelty of the described material, test or use; conversely a patent cannot lower the monograph's standard for a licensed product |
| **ICMR Quality Standards of Indian Medicinal Plants** | A parallel reference for plant quality where the API has no monograph yet |
| **BIS specifications** | Product- and material-specific Indian Standards often run alongside |

## 5. Common quality problems the API is used to catch

1. **Wrong part used** — leaf supplied where the monograph specifies root (a classic substitution with safety consequences).
2. **Species substitution / adulteration** — the identity tests exist precisely for this; vernacular names overlap across genera.
3. **Ash values out of range** — total ash and acid-insoluble ash catch inorganic adulteration and soil contamination.
4. **Moisture and foreign matter** — basic but decisive for shelf life and for microbial load.
5. **Heavy metals and other contaminants** — limits are **prescribed** in the applicable pharmacopoeial/rule text; the point for a product owner is that they must be *tested for and met*, with the current limits read from the current text rather than from a reproduced table.
6. **Microbial limits and pesticide residues** — required by the applicable rules/monographs depending on the product and route of use.
7. **Extractives and assay** — potency drift between batches of a botanical extract.
8. **Missing marker standardisation** — "standardised to X%" is a claim that needs a method, a marker and a batch record.
9. **Finished-formulation gaps** — testing only the raw drug, not the finished product.
10. **Out-of-specification handling** — no documented OOT/OOS investigation, which undermines the whole specification.

## 6. Sourcing a standard when the API has no monograph

Where the API is silent (a new botanical, a regional variety, a novel extract):

1. Check other volumes of the API and the AFI first, and the **Siddha/Unani** pharmacopoeias if the drug appears there.
2. Check the **WHO monographs on selected medicinal plants / benchmarks for herbal medicines**, which are widely used internationally and are accepted as a reference in many dossiers.
3. Check the **Indian Pharmacopoeia**, **European Pharmacopoeia** herbal monographs, or a national pharmacopoeia of the target market.
4. Check **BIS** specifications and the **ICMR Quality Standards of Indian Medicinal Plants**.
5. If nothing official exists, build an in-house specification from published literature and analytical validation, state clearly that it is in-house, and have it accepted by the licensing authority — do not label it "pharmacopoeial".
6. Record the source of every criterion in the product passport — a specification assembled from six references is defensible only if the provenance is written down.

## 7. Documentation a product owner should hold

- [ ] The **exact monograph reference** (Part, volume, page, edition) for every drug substance and every finished formulation.
- [ ] A **specification sheet** that maps each prescribed test to a method, an acceptance criterion and a result.
- [ ] **Batch analytical reports** for each lot, retained with batch manufacturing records.
- [ ] The **certificate of analysis** from the supplier, checked against the monograph rather than accepted as-is.
- [ ] **Identity evidence** (botanical name, part used, source) tied to the biodiversity documentation.
- [ ] **Contaminant testing** records — heavy metals, microbial, pesticide residues — as applicable, against the current limits.
- [ ] A **change-control** record if the API edition or the monograph changes mid-product-life.
- [ ] The **AFI/First Schedule reference** for the formula, kept alongside the API reference.
- [ ] For exports, the **buyer-market reference** (WHO/EuPh/national) and the translation of the specification into that frame.
- [ ] Retention samples and stability data in the storage conditions the monograph prescribes.

## 8. What an Ayurvedic product owner must check

- [ ] Which **API Part and volume** covers my drug substance, and which covers my formulation?
- [ ] Does my **specification include every test** the monograph prescribes — identity, purity, assay, storage?
- [ ] Which **edition** of the monograph am I using, and has it changed since my last batch?
- [ ] Is the **part used** in my raw material the part the monograph specifies?
- [ ] Have **heavy metals, microbial and pesticide** limits been tested and met against the *current* limits?
- [ ] If I claim "standardised", is there a **marker, method and batch record** behind it?
- [ ] If the API has no monograph, is my specification clearly labelled **in-house** with its sources?
- [ ] Do the **AFI/First Schedule** formula reference and the **API** test reference sit together in the dossier?
- [ ] Does the licence file show **Schedule T** compliance for the site making the product?
- [ ] For an **export**, which reference pharmacopoeia does the importing market require?
- [ ] Is the **claims** layer (label, advertisement, website) reviewed separately from the quality layer?
- [ ] Are provenance and verification dates recorded in the product passport for every standard used?
