## 1. Executive Summary

The chapter identifies **authenticity of herbal raw drugs** as a major concern for India's growing herbal sector. It distinguishes between three broad classes of controversial raw drugs:

1. **Recognized substitutes** - alternative plant sources whose identity, relationship to the original drug, and use as substitutes are known and recognized.
2. **Equivalents** - materials from other species that are freely traded or used in place of an accepted Ayurvedic source; the botanical source is often ambiguous after aggregation.
3. **Adulterants** - foreign, incorrect, inferior, or deliberately mixed material that debases an accepted raw drug and may alter its therapeutic and chemical properties.

The chapter attributes much of the problem to the use of **local and trade names rather than standard botanical identities**, especially during wild collection. Once different materials are dried and aggregated, separation can become difficult. The chapter also documents apparent examples of deliberate mixing and possible substitution driven by scarcity, geography, availability, or commercial incentives.

A market-sample observation cited in the chapter reported that **11.5% of herbal raw-drug samples collected from the market were spurious**, attributed to Dr. H. B. Singh, Chief Scientist, NISCAIR (Retd.), during a National Workshop in Delhi on 28 May 2015. This is reported as a personal communication in the source, not as an independently documented statistical estimate.

The chapter's proposed responses are:

- use **standard binomial botanical nomenclature** for trade and use;
- create an illustrated **Compendium of Traded Medicinal Plants in India** containing botanical names, local names, trade names, API names, descriptions and photographs;
- maintain **chain-of-custody / backward-linkage information** from primary production through the value chain;
- update the **Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India (API)** to include traditionally used equivalents and substitutes.

## 2. Why Herbal Raw-Drug Authenticity Is a Problem

The chapter explains that the older model in which an herbal healer personally collected plant material has increasingly been replaced by commercially manufactured formulations requiring bulk raw-material procurement from multiple regions. The responsibility for correct identification has therefore shifted toward large numbers of wild gatherers and intermediaries, many of whom may not be proficient in botanical identification.

The source reports that:

- commercially traded material may contain material from **more than one plant species** while being sold as a single raw drug;
- replacement raw drugs are increasingly used where the authentic source is unavailable in the required quantity;
- wild collection is often organized using **local names rather than botanical identity**;
- aggregation, drying and downstream handling can make later separation or authentication difficult.

The chapter uses the term **`sandigdha dravya`** for controversial herbal raw drugs.

## 3. Taxonomy of Controversial Herbal Raw Drugs

| Category | Source meaning | Botanical identity | Typical reason / mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Substitute (`pratinidhi dravya`)** | Accepted alternative used when the original drug is unavailable or difficult to obtain | Generally known for both original and substitute | Scarcity, population decline, geography, or availability |
| **Equivalent** | Material treated as equivalent to an accepted raw drug and used/traded under the same drug name | Often ambiguous, especially after aggregation | Similar appearance, same/similar genus, regional practice, perceived similarity of properties, or geographic availability |
| **Adulterant** | Foreign, incorrect, inferior, non-accepted or deliberately mixed material sold as the accepted drug | Incorrect and/or intentionally concealed | Collection error, similar appearance, name confusion, economic motive, deliberate mixing |

### Important distinction from the chapter

The chapter stresses that **recognized substitutes are not equivalent to adulterants**. A recognized substitute has a known plant source and an accepted rationale for replacement. Equivalents and adulterants are more problematic because the source material may be ambiguous and because adulteration may reduce authenticity and quality.

## 4. Recognized Substitutes

### 4.1 General principle

The chapter describes `pratinidhi dravya` or substitute use as permissible in circumstances such as scarcity or geographic unavailability, provided the identity of the original and substitute is known.

Identification and validation of a substitute is described as a prolonged process involving comparison of properties such as:

- **Rasa**
- **Guna**
- **Virya**
- **Vipaka**

The source notes that a substitute may come from a different species in the same family or even from a different family. Classical works cited include **Bhavaprakasha**, **Yogaratnakara**, and **Bhaishajya Ratnavali**.

### 4.2 Examples explicitly given

| Original / accepted drug | Recognized substitute | Botanical identity / note |
|---|---|---|
| Kshir Kakoli | Ashwagandha | `Lilium polyphyllum` -> `Withania somnifera` |
| Kakoli | Ashwagandha | The chapter describes the broader Ashtavarga substitution practice and depicts `Roscoea purpurea` as Kakoli in the figure; the text separately notes Ashwagandha as substitute for Kakoli/Kshir Kakoli in practice |
| Avisa / Ativisha | Mustaka | `Aconitum heterophyllum` -> `Cyperus rotundus` |
| Murva | Jhinghan | `Marsdenia tenacissima` -> `Lannea coromandelica` |
| Chavya | Pippal Mool | `Piper chaba` -> `Piper longum` |
| Dhanvayasah | Yavasaka | `Fagonia cretica` -> `Alhagi pseudalhagi` |
| Riddhi / Vriddhi | Varahi Kand | The chapter associates these substitutions with `Dioscorea bulbifera` |

### 4.3 Ashtavarga substitution example

The chapter notes that the complete **Ashtavarga** group of Himalayan underground plant drugs is often substituted in practice, including:

- Kakoli / Kshir Kakoli -> Ashwagandha (`Withania somnifera`)
- Riddhi / Vriddhi -> Varahi Kand (`Dioscorea bulbifera`)

The source states that widespread use of accepted substitutes can become so common that the original raw drug becomes almost forgotten.

## 5. Equivalents

The chapter describes equivalents as a major source of ambiguity. Two broad patterns are identified.

### 5.1 Equivalents from similar-looking or related species

These materials are collected from other species, often within the same genus, and freely used under the same drug name.

#### Daruharidra

- Officially correlated in the chapter with **`Berberis aristata`**.
- Other `Berberis` species growing in the same habitat are also collected and used as `Daruharidra` equivalents.
- A figure identifies: `Berberis aristata`, `B. asiatica`, `B. chitria`, `B. lycium`, and `Mahonia` sp. in the context of the `Daruharidra` trade.

#### Gokshura

- Official source: **`Tribulus terrestris`**.
- Other `Tribulus` species are also collected and traded as `Gokshura`.
- The figure shows `T. terrestris`, fruits of `T. terrestris`, `T. alatus`, `T. lanuginosus`, and `Pedalium murex` fruits/plant material under the broader `Gokshura` trade context.

#### Bala

- Official source described in the chapter: **`Sida rhombifolia`**.
- Other `Sida` species are also collected and used as `Bala`.

#### Bhumi Amla

- Official source described: **`Phyllanthus amarus`**.
- Several other herbaceous `Phyllanthus` species are collected and used under the same trade name.

#### Shankpushpi

The chapter identifies `Shankpushpi` as another example in which multiple plant species are traded under one name. A figure shows:

- `Clitoria ternatea`
- `Convolvulus prostratus`
- `Convolvulus arvensis`
- `Evolvulus alsinoides`

### 5.2 Equivalents from unrelated or geographically different plants

The chapter also records use of different plant sources unrelated to the official source, including:

| Trade / Ayurvedic name | Material reported in use |
|---|---|
| `Jivanthi` / `Jivanti` | `Holostemma ada-kodien`, `Flickingeria mackraei`, `Leptadenia reticulata` |
| `Rasna` in North India | `Pluchea lanceolata` |
| `Rasna` in South India | `Alpinia galanga` as a geographic equivalent |
| `Parpata` / `Parpataka` | `Fumaria indica`, `Fumaria officinalis`, `Polycarpaea corymbosa`, `Oldenlandia corymbosa`, `Mollugo cerviana` |
| `Chireta` / `Chiretta` | `Swertia chirayita`; `Swertia cordata` is described as a recognized substitute; other `Swertia` species and allied plants are also traded as `Chireta` |
| `Daruharidra` | Species of `Berberis`, `Mahonia`, and `Coscinium` are described as being used because the plants contain berberine as a major organic compound |

### 5.3 Chireta case

The chapter provides an unusually detailed example:

- Official source: **`Swertia chirayita`**.
- Recognized substitute mentioned: **`Swertia cordata`**.
- Other material traded as Chireta may come from other `Swertia` species, `Helinia elliptica`, and `Enicostemma axillare`.
- `Andrographis paniculata` may also be mixed with or clubbed with true Chireta material under trade usage such as `kalmegh` / `tikta-chireta`.
- `Pant (2005)` is cited for nine different `Swertia` species traded as Chireta in Nepal.
- `Khanal et al. (2014)` is cited for twelve `Swertia` species traded as Chiraito in Nepal, with adulteration reported as high as 20%.
- The chapter states that Nepal prohibits wild collection and trade of `Swertia chirayita` from May through September and that more than 80% of Chireta collected from Nepal is exported to India.

### 5.4 Chemical-similarity argument

The chapter notes that common medicinal properties or similar active principles are sometimes used to rationalize unrelated species as equivalents. Example:

- `Berberis`, `Mahonia` and `Coscinium` are all used as `Daruharidra` in some contexts because the source states they contain **berberine** as a major organic compound.

The chapter explicitly cautions that many such equivalents **have not been officially recognized by the Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India (API)**.

## 6. Adulterants

The chapter characterizes adulterants as the most concerning category. Adulteration ranges from accidental contamination/mixing to deliberate addition of foreign or inferior material for profit.

### 6.1 Types of adulteration described

- Accidental mixing by inexperienced wild gatherers collecting similar-looking plants.
- Mixing of non-accepted parts of the same plant.
- Mixing of foreign plant material.
- Deliberate mixing of inferior material.
- Entire lots being effectively spurious.
- Name-based confusion where botanically different material is sold under the same trade name.

The chapter states that adulteration can adversely affect the **therapeutic and chemical properties** of the accepted herbal raw drug.

### 6.2 Documented examples

| Accepted / expected raw drug | Adulterant / substituted material reported | Source context |
|---|---|---|
| `Pippali` (`Piper longum`) | Fruiting spikes of other `Piper` species | Market samples; seemingly deliberate mixing noted |
| `Gurhal phool` (`Hibiscus rosa-sinensis`) | Flowers of `Rhododendron arboreum` | Herbal mandi survey; name similarity and different source region |
| `Brahmi` (`Centella asiatica`) | Leaves of `Malva rotundifolia`, `Malva pusilla`, `Merremia emarginata`, `Evolvulus nummularis` | Reported by Dr. H. B. Singh based on market-sample work |
| `Rakta punarnava` (`Boerhavia diffusa`) | `Trianthema portulacastrum` | Reported by Dr. H. B. Singh |
| `Rasna` (`Pluchea lanceolata`) | `Blepharispermum subsessile` | Reported from Jagdalpur, Dhamtari and Madhya Pradesh mandis |
| `Musli` | Roots of `Murdannia edulis` (`Aneilema scapiflora`) | Reported as trade practice |
| `Ashoka chhal` (`Saraca asoca`) | Possible bark of `Polyalthia longifolia`, `Bauhinia variegata`, `Trema orientalis`, `Shorea robusta` | Source describes this as suspicion requiring investigation |
| `Atees` (`Aconitum heterophyllum`) | `Cryptocoryne spiralis` corms; `Chaerophyllum villosum` roots | Chennai / Delhi market observations |

> The source uses both the terms “substitute” and “adulterant” in discussions of `Atees` depending on the material and context. These classifications should not be collapsed into a single category when using this document for retrieval.

## 7. Detailed Adulteration / Authenticity Cases

### 7.1 Gurhal phool vs. Burash phool

- Expected raw drug: flowers of **`Hibiscus rosa-sinensis`**, known in the chapter as `Gurhal phool`.
- Material observed in trade as `Gurhal phool`: flowers of **`Rhododendron arboreum`**.
- `Rhododendron arboreum` flowers are locally known as `Burash phool` in the described supply region.
- The chapter traces major supplies of the material sold as Gurhal phool to **Chamba district, Himachal Pradesh**.
- The source states that similarity of local names appears to explain the mix-up and also explains large differences in market prices.

### 7.2 Brahmi adulteration

The chapter reports market-sample information attributed to **Dr. H. B. Singh**, including mixing of leaves from:

- `Malva rotundifolia`
- `Malva pusilla`
- `Merremia emarginata`
- `Evolvulus nummularis`

with **`Centella asiatica`** (`Brahmi`).

### 7.3 Rakta Punarnava adulteration

The same source-attributed market-sample evidence reports adulteration of **`Boerhavia diffusa`** (`Rakta Punarnava`) with **`Trianthema portulacastrum`**.

### 7.4 Rasna adulteration

The chapter cites earlier work reporting **`Blepharispermum subsessile`** being traded as `Rasna` instead of accepted **`Pluchea lanceolata`** in herbal mandis in Chhattisgarh and Madhya Pradesh.

### 7.5 Ashoka Chhal authenticity problem

The chapter highlights a major supply-identity puzzle:

- `Ashoka chhal` is expected to be bark of **`Saraca asoca`**.
- The domestic herbal industry is described as using about **2,000 MT annually** for ASU formulations.
- Monitoring of wild populations in Kerala, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Goa and Odisha did not indicate significant collection.
- The species was not reported as being under significant commercial cultivation.
- The chapter therefore questions the authenticity of the material sold as Ashoka bark and leaves the source unresolved.
- The chapter explicitly frames this as a **suspicion requiring investigation**, not as proof that the cited substitute species are the actual source.

### 7.6 Atees / Ativisha

- Authentic drug: tuberous roots of **`Aconitum heterophyllum`**.
- Price range stated in the chapter: approximately **Rs. 3,000-4,000/kg**.
- Recognized substitute: `Musta` (`Cyperus rotundus`).
- Chennai observation: corms of **`Cryptocoryne spiralis`** traded under `Atividayam` / `Atividyam` as an Atees substitute, around **Rs. 200-300/kg**.
- Kerala terminology noted: `Nattavitayam`.
- A cited author, Nair (2004), argued that `Cryptocoryne spiralis` should be treated as an **adulterant** because it is taxonomically very distant from `Aconitum heterophyllum`.
- Delhi observation: roots of **`Chaerophyllum villosum`** sold as another Atees adulterant under `Mithi-Paash`.

## 8. Banslochan / Tabasheer, Gandhapura Patra Taila and Kapur

### 8.1 Banslochan / Tabasheer

The chapter says the Indian herbal industry uses an estimated **2,000 MT/year** of Banslochan / Tabasheer in Ayurvedic, Siddha and Unani formulations.

**Expected material:** a translucent whitish substance, sometimes bluish, consisting mainly of silica and water with traces of lime and potash, obtained from the nodal joints of bamboo species such as:

- `Bambusa bambos`
- `Bambusa balcooa`
- `Melocanna bambusoides`

**Authenticity concern:**

- The study found no record of wild collection of the material in India.
- Extensive field surveys did not observe such collection.
- Foreign-trade analysis did not reveal imports large enough to account for the stated consumption.
- Large quantities nevertheless continue to be traded under names including `banslochan asli`, `vanshlochan singapuri`, `banslochan desi`, `tawasheer`, and `bamboo-manna`.
- Reported prices vary from **less than Rs. 100/kg to more than Rs. 10,000/kg**.
- Informal interactions with traders and experts led to the source's suspicion that much of the material sold as Banslochan **may not be a plant product at all**.

### 8.2 Gandhapura Patra Taila and Kapur

The chapter similarly discusses:

- **Gandhapura Patra Taila** - oil associated with `Gaultheria fragranissima` and `Gaultheria procumbens`.
- **Kapur** - oil associated with `Cinnamomum camphora`.

The study could not locate corresponding large-scale harvest records for the cited plant sources or import records sufficient to explain the trade volumes. The source therefore states that the material used under these names **may not be of plant origin** and calls for investigation and resolution.

## 9. Why Local and Trade Names Create Authentication Risk

The chapter's central argument is that **one vernacular or trade name can refer to multiple botanical species**, and the same botanical species can have multiple local names.

Examples:

| Trade / local name | Botanical identity or identities mentioned |
|---|---|
| Guduchi | `Tinospora cordifolia`; local names include `amrutha`, `giloe`, `gulje`, `amritahballi` |
| Ratanjot | `Arnebia benthamii`; in Rajasthan `Jatropha curcas` is also called Ratanjot |
| Jivanti / Jivanti | `Holostemma ada-kodien`, `Flickingeria mackraei`, `Leptadenia reticulata` |
| Rasna | `Pluchea lanceolata` in North India; `Alpinia galanga` in South India as geographic equivalent |
| Daruharidra | `Berberis aristata`; other `Berberis`, `Mahonia`, `Coscinium` materials also used |
| Gokshura | Official source `Tribulus terrestris`; multiple other `Tribulus` and related materials also used |
| Chireta | `Swertia chirayita` plus multiple `Swertia` and allied species in trade |

The chapter's `Jivanti` example illustrates a specific failure mode: traders may assign a scientific name to material based on a publication or prior textual correlation even when the actual traded specimen belongs to another plant.

## 12. Proposed Solutions in the Source

### A. Standard botanical nomenclature

The chapter recommends that trade and use of herbal raw drugs be conducted under **standard binomial botanical nomenclature**.

It proposes an illustrated **Compendium of Traded Medicinal Plants in India** containing:

- plant descriptions;
- updated botanical nomenclature;
- authentic local names;
- trade names;
- API names;
- photographs of raw drugs;
- photographs of plant sources.

The chapter estimates that such a compendium could require **2-3 years** of intensive table work and field visits.

### B. Chain of custody / backward linkage

The source recommends attaching **backward-linkage / chain-of-custody information** to herbal raw drugs through the supply chain, from primary production to downstream value-chain nodes, so that identity can be investigated whenever doubt arises.

### C. Update the Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India

The chapter recommends that the **API be updated to include traditionally used equivalents and substitutes**.

## 13. RAG Retrieval Chunks

### Chunk 09-01 - Core problem and authenticity
**Keywords:** authenticity, herbal raw drugs, controversial drugs, sandigdha dravya, wild collection, local names, botanical identity, spurious samples

The chapter identifies authenticity as a foundational issue for the herbal sector. Commercialization has shifted raw-material collection away from individually knowledgeable healers toward large-scale wild gathering and procurement. This increases the risk that multiple species are aggregated under one trade name or that replacement materials are used. The source cites a personal communication reporting 11.5% spurious market samples.

### Chunk 09-02 - Substitute definition
**Keywords:** substitute, pratinidhi dravya, Kshir Kakoli, Ashwagandha, Ashtavarga, Rasa, Guna, Virya, Vipaka

Recognized substitutes are alternatives used when the original drug is unavailable because of scarcity or geography. The source states that the original and substitute plant identities are known and that validation may compare rasa, guna, virya and vipaka. Example: `Withania somnifera` as a substitute for `Lilium polyphyllum`.

### Chunk 09-03 - Common accepted substitutions
**Keywords:** Mustaka, Avisa, Jhinghan, Murva, Pippal Mool, Chavya, Yavasaka, Dhanvayasah, Fagonia, Alhagi

The chapter lists accepted substitutions such as `Cyperus rotundus` for `Aconitum heterophyllum`, `Lannea coromandelica` for `Marsdenia tenacissima`, `Piper longum` for `Piper chaba`, and `Alhagi pseudalhagi` for `Fagonia cretica`.

### Chunk 09-04 - Equivalents from related species
**Keywords:** equivalents, Daruharidra, Berberis, Gokshura, Tribulus, Bala, Sida, Bhumi Amla, Phyllanthus, Shankpushpi

The source describes use of multiple species under a single Ayurvedic or trade name. Official sources may be species-specific, but other related species are collected and marketed as equivalents. Once material is aggregated, species-level separation may become impossible.

### Chunk 09-05 - Geographic and unrelated equivalents
**Keywords:** Jivanti, Rasna, Parpata, Chireta, Chiraito, Nepal, Andrographis, Enicostemma

Different plants can serve as regional or unrelated equivalents. `Rasna` may refer to `Pluchea lanceolata` in North India and `Alpinia galanga` in South India. `Jivanti` has several reported plant sources. `Chireta` may involve multiple `Swertia` species and allied plants.

### Chunk 09-06 - Adulteration mechanisms
**Keywords:** adulterant, deliberate mixing, accidental mixing, Pippali, Piper, Gurhal, Rhododendron, Hibiscus

Adulteration ranges from accidental collection of similar-looking plants to deliberate mixing of inferior or foreign material. Examples include other `Piper` fruiting spikes mixed with `Piper longum` and `Rhododendron arboreum` flowers sold as `Hibiscus rosa-sinensis`.

### Chunk 09-07 - Market examples
**Keywords:** Brahmi, Rakta Punarnava, Rasna, Musli, Ashoka Chhal, Saraca asoca

The chapter reports market adulteration involving `Centella asiatica`, `Boerhavia diffusa`, `Pluchea lanceolata`, and `Saraca asoca`. Some claims are attributed to named researchers or earlier publications; suspected Ashoka substitutes are not presented as proven.

### Chunk 09-08 - Atees / Ativisha
**Keywords:** Atees, Ativisha, Aconitum heterophyllum, Musta, Cryptocoryne spiralis, Chaerophyllum villosum

`Aconitum heterophyllum` is the accepted Atees source described. `Cyperus rotundus` is a recognized substitute, while `Cryptocoryne spiralis` and `Chaerophyllum villosum` are discussed as market substitutions/adulterants.

### Chunk 09-09 - Banslochan authenticity puzzle
**Keywords:** Banslochan, Tabasheer, bamboo, 2000 MT, silica, adulteration, non-plant origin

The chapter estimates 2,000 MT annual use of Banslochan but reports no observed wild collection or sufficiently large imports. Large quantities are nevertheless traded across highly variable prices. The source suspects that much of the traded material may not be plant-derived and calls for investigation.

### Chunk 09-10 - Local names and identity errors
**Keywords:** Guduchi, Ratanjot, Tinospora, Arnebia, Jatropha, Jivanti, Flickingeria, trade names

Regional naming causes botanical misidentification. `Guduchi` has several local names. `Ratanjot` can refer to different plants in different regions. The Jivanti example shows how traders can incorrectly map a traded specimen to the botanical name in a text.

### Chunk 09-11 - Recommended governance / data model
**Keywords:** botanical nomenclature, compendium, chain of custody, API, traceability, taxonomy, authentication

The proposed system-level response is standardized binomial nomenclature, an illustrated compendium with local/trade/API names, chain-of-custody traceability from source to end user, and an updated API incorporating traditionally used equivalents and substitutes.

## 15. Source Limitations and Interpretation Rules

1. Some observations are **field observations**, while others are **literature reports or personal communications**.
2. Several cases are described as **suspected**, **likely**, or **requiring investigation**. These should remain uncertain in downstream retrieval and should not be converted into definitive facts.
3. Botanical names and common/trade names are not interchangeable. A RAG system should keep them as separate entities linked by explicit relationships such as `official_source`, `recognized_substitute`, `reported_equivalent`, `reported_adulterant`, and `trade_name`.
4. Where the chapter says a classification is debated - for example, whether a material is a substitute or adulterant - preserve the distinction and the cited interpretation rather than forcing a single label.
5. This chapter is a **source document about the state of herbal raw-drug trade/authentication as described in the study**, not a current regulatory standard or a modern pharmacopoeial verdict.
