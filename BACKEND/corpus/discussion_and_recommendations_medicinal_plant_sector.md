# Discussion and Recommendations

## Source context

This chapter is Chapter 12, **“Discussion and Recommendations,”** from *Medicinal Plants: An Assessment of their Demand and Supply in India*. It synthesizes the preceding assessment of India's medicinal-plant sector and proposes actions concerning wild harvesting, cultivation, post-harvest handling, trade traceability, research and development, policy and regulation, capacity building, and institutional mechanisms.

> **Source-era note:** Quantitative findings refer primarily to **2014-15**, while many recommended implementation dates run from **2016/2017 through 2030**. These dates are historical recommendations from the source and should not be interpreted as evidence that the proposed actions were subsequently implemented.

## Executive summary

The source estimates the domestic herbal industry's turnover at **₹20,000 crore in 2014-15**, representing approximately **11% annual growth over the preceding ten years**. Estimated annual herbal raw-drug trade volume increased from **317,500 MT in 2004-05 to 512,000 MT in 2014-15**, while estimated trade value increased from **₹1,070 crore to ₹7,000 crore** over the same period.

The chapter identifies an underlying tension: the herbal sector has substantial growth potential, but increasing demand is accompanied by concerns about **sustainable harvesting, authenticity, quality, fair trade, traceability, fragmented regulation, weak data systems, and inadequate research coordination**.

The central recommendations are to:

1. conserve threatened wild medicinal-plant resources in their natural habitats and strengthen them through community participation;
2. promote commercial cultivation where wild supply is inadequate or authentic cultivated material commands a premium;
3. improve post-harvest infrastructure and handling practices;
4. establish transparent trade records and chain-of-custody mechanisms;
5. strengthen coordinated medicinal-plant R&D and reference repositories;
6. establish a national medicinal-plant policy/strategy and harmonize data and transit systems;
7. build stakeholder capacity; and
8. strengthen the National Medicinal Plants Board (NMPB) through regional technical support groups and a national data portal.

# 12.1 Herbal sector in India on a path of growth

## 12.1.1 Growth indicators

| Indicator | 2004-05 | 2014-15 | Source-stated change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Estimated annual herbal raw-drug trade volume | 317,500 MT | 512,000 MT | Increased substantially |
| Estimated herbal raw-drug trade value | ₹1,070 crore | ₹7,000 crore | Increased substantially |
| Trade value of herbal raw drugs consumed by domestic herbal industry | Baseline year | 2014-15 | Approximately 3-fold increase over ten years |
| Export-related trade value | Baseline year | 2014-15 | Approximately 9-fold increase over ten years |
| Estimated domestic herbal-industry turnover | — | ₹20,000 crore | Approximately 11% annual growth over 2004-05 to 2014-15 |

The chapter states that the domestic herbal industry had an estimated turnover of **₹20,000 crore in 2014-15**. It also describes India as having potential for further expansion because of its traditional use of medicinal plants, diverse agro-climatic zones, established cultivation, herbal raw-drug markets, experienced traders, and research organizations.

## 12.1.2 Strengths

The source identifies the following strengths:

- A strong and continuing traditional use of plants for healthcare and wellbeing.
- Diverse agro-climatic conditions supporting a rich medicinal-plant resource base.
- Existing cultivation of many medicinal-plant species and farmer interest in suitable species.
- Strong herbal raw-drug **mandis** and experienced herbal traders linking primary producers with end users.
- A substantial network of research organizations working on medicinal plants and herbal raw drugs.

## 12.1.3 Weaknesses

The source identifies:

- Declining populations of many wild-collected medicinal plants because of unorganized wild collection.
- Weak post-harvest handling protocols, causing quality loss and damage.
- Complex state-wise regulatory systems covering wild collection, cultivation, transport, and trade.
- Apparently non-transparent trade practices and absence of centralized mandi-level trade records along the trade chain.
- Increasing concerns over quality and authenticity of herbal raw drugs used in formulations.
- An inadequate **ITC (HS)** system for maintaining species-wise export records.
- Lack of a national medicinal-plants policy, leaving organizations to operate largely on independent agendas.

## 12.1.4 Opportunities

The source identifies:

- Expected continued global demand for herbal products for healthcare and lifestyle-related uses.
- Programs by **CBOs/NGOs** to organize wild gatherers and farmers, promote sustainable harvest/cultivation, and develop fair-trade processes.
- More than **8,600 registered domestic herbal units** beginning to adopt good manufacturing practices, including annual recording of herbal raw-drug consumption.
- Government support for medicinal-plant conservation and cultivation programs intended to strengthen the herbal raw-drug resource base.

## 12.1.5 Threats

The source identifies:

- A continuation of existing practices without change could weaken confidence in the herbal sector.
- Delay in adopting a national medicinal-plant policy could reduce India's initiative in the growing global herbal sector.
- Development of synthetic drugs could reduce demand for medicinal species under large-scale cultivation.
- Existing government policies governing harvesting, domestic use, and export could hamper primary production and consumption of plant material.

# 12.2 Recommendations

The recommendations below are organized according to the source's eight thematic areas.

---

## 12.2.1 Management of wild resources

### Source rationale

The chapter states that wild-collected medicinal plants remain a mainstay of herbal healthcare because they are accessible to local populations for self-use and trade. Over-harvesting and inadequate management have placed many species under serious threat. The source specifically identifies **trees, other long-gestation species, and Himalayan herbs** as vulnerable groups.

The recommended conservation approach is primarily **in situ conservation** in natural habitats. The chapter points to **Medicinal Plant Conservation Areas (MPCAs)** and **Medicinal Plant Conservation and Development Areas (MPCDAs)** as mechanisms intended to conserve threatened populations, while noting that their status should be evaluated.

Because local communities have rights over minor forest produce, the source argues that conservation efforts should involve communities together with State Forest Departments, research organizations, and local CBOs/NGOs. **Joint Forest Management (JFM)** is identified as a mechanism that could support **Community Medicinal Plant Reserves (CMPRs)** for sustainable harvesting of threatened species on forest/common land.

### Recommended action matrix

| Rec. | Recommendation | Main action points | Suggested period in source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Country-wide program to strengthen wild populations of medicinal trees, long-gestation species, and high-Himalayan herbs through CMPRs/JFM | Engage suitable CBOs/NGOs; organize and train JFM committees; strengthen medicinal-tree resources; expand in-situ conservation networks | 2017; 2017-2026; 2017-2026 |
| B | Assess threat status and prioritize conservation | Identify national anchor agency; establish expert group; fund threat-assessment workshops and Red Lists | 2017; 2017; 2017-2022 |
| C | Conduct population surveys for Red-listed species | Identify national and regional agencies; fund surveys; equip State Forest Departments for in-situ conservation/resource enhancement | 2017; 2017-2022; 2019-2030 |
| D | Develop forest-type-specific management/silviculture systems | Assign research work to forestry research organizations such as ICFRE with financial support | 2016-2030 |
| E | Establish long-term monitoring/evaluation of MPCAs/MPCDAs | Periodic monitoring; evaluate conservation status; prepare triennial status reports and address gaps | 2016-2030; reporting 2020-2030 |

### RAG facts

- **MPCAs** = Medicinal Plant Conservation Areas.
- **MPCDAs** = Medicinal Plant Conservation and Development Areas.
- **CMPRs** = Community Medicinal Plant Reserves.
- **JFM** = Joint Forest Management.
- The source recommends community participation as a core condition for successful conservation of wild medicinal plants.

## 12.2.2 Promotion of commercial cultivation of medicinal plants

### Source rationale

Cultivation is presented as necessary where adequate wild supplies are becoming difficult to obtain and where authentic material receives a premium.

The chapter states that **NMPB** had been promoting commercial medicinal-plant cultivation for approximately 15 years and, at the time of the source study, supported cultivation of **116 listed species under three subsidy regimes**.

The study's stated experience was that cultivation could be introduced successfully in new areas when local CBOs/NGOs helped organize farmer clusters and provided technical and marketing support, with or without subsidy.

The source identifies several obstacles:

- inadequate authentic, good-quality seed/planting material;
- insufficient field demonstration of integrated cultivation with conventional crops; and
- difficulty in accessing later subsidy instalments because of complex inspection procedures.

The source recommends continuing subsidy support, especially for newly introduced Red-listed species with higher risk of low returns, while revisiting the subsidy-delivery mechanism.

### Recommended action matrix

| Rec. | Recommendation | Main action points | Suggested period in source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Develop high-quality planting material | Assign production of certified planting material to selected research organizations; establish institutional medicinal-plant nurseries | 2017-18; 2018-2025 |
| B | Organize farmers through CBOs/NGOs | Select organizations at roughly one per 3-4 districts; train them; develop clusters of at least 1,000 farmers in allocated districts; support cultivation promotion | 2016-17; 2017-18; 2018-2027 |
| C | Certify cultivated herbal raw-drug material | Identify a nodal agency; develop certification scope/protocols; pilot certification in 4-5 village clusters | 2017-18; 2018-2020 |
| D | Demonstrate agroforestry/intercropping models | Run on-farm demonstrations integrating medicinal plants with conventional crops; pilot 10 species across agro-climatic zones | 2017-2026 |
| E | Improve subsidy delivery | Move toward farmer-cluster-based and production-linked subsidy mechanisms; conduct national consultation | 2017-18 |

## 12.2.3 Good post-harvest handling practices and infrastructure

### Source findings

The chapter reports substantial wastage and quality loss caused by poor post-harvest handling. It considers this important both for wild harvest and cultivation: losses from wild material affect the resource, while losses from cultivated material reduce farmer returns.

The source says farmers and wild gatherers reported limited awareness of good post-harvest handling protocols. It also notes an acute shortage of simple infrastructure such as:

- driers;
- drying yards;
- covered drying platforms;
- weighing equipment;
- storage/godown facilities; and
- nearby laboratories for quality testing.

Rain can cause fresh plant material to be stored indoors in bundles, leading to fungal infestation. Dried material may remain in poor packing until traders collect it. Lack of local laboratories can make producers vulnerable to quality disputes and low-quality assessments made farther along the trade chain.

### Recommended action matrix

| Rec. | Recommendation | Main action points | Suggested period in source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Establish post-harvest facilities in key growing clusters | Select local CBOs/NGOs; create at least **100 post-harvest handling centres** equipped with chopping machines, drying courtyards, covered platforms, solar dryers, weighing scales and godowns; train farmers | 2017; 2017-2020; 2017-2022 |
| B | Deploy mobile laboratories | Pilot mobile quality-testing laboratories with necessary equipment and trained technicians at farm gate before dispatch to bulk buyers | 2017-18 |

## 12.2.4 Fair trade practices and traceability

### Source assessment

The chapter describes the herbal raw-drug trade as far from transparent. It identifies weak mechanisms for:

- following material through the trade chain;
- linking traded material to its phytogeographic production source;
- maintaining trade data for conventional herbal mandis;
- establishing backward linkages to confirm authenticity; and
- demonstrating fair returns to primary producers.

The source highlights unresolved questions concerning the origin and authenticity of materials traded under names such as:

- **Jivanti**;
- **Vidari**;
- **Shankhapushpi**; and
- **Daruharidra**.

It also repeats the earlier question cited from Ved and Goraya (2008): **“from where does Asoka Chhal come”**, using it as an illustration of the traceability problem.

### Recommended action matrix

| Rec. | Recommendation | Main action points | Suggested period in source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Establish a trade-record management system for conventional herbal raw-drug mandis | Develop prototype; conduct national consultation; finalize the system | 2017-18; 2018-19 |
| B | Develop and implement chain-of-custody protocols | Develop prototype; conduct national consultation; finalize protocols | 2017-18; 2018-19 |
| C | Build capacity for species identification and chain of custody | Train Panchayat functionaries and State Forest Department personnel; develop training/reference material | 2017-18; 2018-22 |

### Core RAG relationship

`Primary source / wild or cultivated producer` → `collection/handling` → `trader / mandi` → `processor / herbal unit / exporter` → `end user`

The source argues that the chain needs record-keeping and traceability mechanisms that connect traded material back toward its biological and geographic source.

## 12.2.5 Research and development

### Source assessment

The chapter states that medicinal-plant R&D has historically been too scattered, small, short-lived, and poorly coordinated. Different organizations may pursue independent agendas, creating duplication and limited coordination.

Priority research areas identified in the source include:

- resolving botanical-identity issues in controversial raw-drug groups such as **Jivanti, Daruharidra, Shankhapushpi, and Vidari**;
- improving availability of raw drugs from species such as **Kakarshingi**;
- developing high-yielding germplasm for species proposed for cultivation;
- establishing herbal raw-drug repositories for reference/authentication; and
- establishing durable review systems for medicinal-plant demand and supply.

### Recommended action matrix

| Rec. | Recommendation | Main action points | Suggested period in source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Develop a coordinated network of medicinal-plant research agencies | Designate national nodal agency; connect research organizations; develop common research agenda; hold annual review/planning seminars | 2017; 2017-2018; 2017-2026 |
| B | Resolve controversial raw-drug identity issues | Study disputed botanical identities; develop field-character-based identification keys | 2017-2026; 2017-2020 |
| C | Establish traded herbal raw-drug repositories | Prepare terms of reference for one central and 4-5 regional repositories; assign development to experienced research organizations | 2016-17; 2017-2022 |
| D | Conserve seeds of Red-listed species | Establish long-term arrangements with NBPGR or comparable facilities; collect seed from different provenances for genetic-diversity conservation | 2017; 2017-2026 |
| E | Conduct triennial demand-and-supply reviews | Appoint long-term national nodal agency; review domestic industry, trade, households, folk healers/ISM practitioners, wild supply, cultivation, trade, and policy | 2017-2026 |

### Demand-and-supply review scope

The source proposes periodic assessment of:

1. domestic herbal-industry consumption;
2. species-wise imports and exports, using DGCIS data, key-port DTR records, and exporter/importer interactions;
3. household consumption;
4. consumption by folk healers and ISM practitioners;
5. wild-collected supply from forests and non-forest landscapes;
6. cultivated supply;
7. herbal raw-drug trade; and
8. policy and legislative factors affecting the sector.

## 12.2.6 Policy and regulatory regime

### Source assessment

The chapter attributes lack of sector-wide vision and focus to the absence of a national medicinal-plants policy/strategy. Different organizations were described as pursuing independent agendas without a mechanism for national coordination.

The source also identifies:

- weak data management for wild-collected medicinal plants after devolution of rights over minor forest produce;
- complex and state-specific forest-produce transit rules;
- deterioration of herbal material when consignments stop repeatedly at State borders;
- concerns about notifications under **Section 38 of the Biological Diversity Act, 2002** and the interaction between restrictions and cultivation promotion;
- a need to review the list of **Normally Traded Commodities** under the Biological Diversity Act; and
- inadequate specificity of **ITC (HS)** codes for species-wise medicinal-plant foreign-trade management.

The source gives **Kuth** as an example where historical cultivation helped preserve wild populations and notes that commercial demand for Kuth was, at the time of the study, fully met from cultivation.

### Recommended action matrix

| Rec. | Recommendation | Main action points | Suggested period in source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Develop and adopt a national medicinal-plant policy/strategy | Identify responsible organization; national consultation; prepare draft; seek public views; adopt policy | 2017-18; 2018-19 |
| B | Improve data management for wild harvests | Review relevant policies/legislation and incorporate data procedures; consult nationally; finalize and circulate procedures | 2017-18; 2018-19; 2019-20 |
| C | Harmonize Forest Produce Transit Rules | Compile state rules; develop prototype National Transit Rules; consult nationally; finalize and circulate | 2017-18; 2018-19; 2019-20 |
| D | Review notifications under the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 | Engage National Biodiversity Authority; review Section 38 notifications and Normally Traded Commodities; convert strict-regulation lists into “Action Lists” | 2016-17; 2017-18 |
| E | Improve the ITC (HS) system for herbal raw drugs in foreign trade | Map herbal raw-drug entities to botanical identities; develop species-wise coding for exports/imports | 2017-19; 2019-20 |

### Key policy concepts

- **National medicinal-plant policy/strategy:** proposed national coordinating framework.
- **National Transit Rules:** proposed harmonized approach replacing fragmented state transit rules.
- **Action Lists:** proposed proactive framework for species otherwise subject to strict regulation, combining conservation, cultivation, and research objectives.
- **Species-wise ITC (HS) coding:** proposed mechanism to improve visibility of medicinal-plant foreign trade.

## 12.2.7 Capacity building

### Source assessment

The chapter identifies insufficient stakeholder awareness as a contributor to sector problems. Examples include:

- Panchayat functionaries responsible for transit passes lacking awareness of data-management requirements;
- traders and Customs officials lacking sufficient awareness of botanical correlations of trade entities; and
- wild gatherers lacking awareness of the impacts of destructive harvesting.

The source therefore recommends awareness and capacity-building programs, illustrated materials on sustainable harvesting and good post-harvest handling, and an **Illustrated Compendium of Traded Medicinal Plants in India**.

### Recommended action matrix

| Rec. | Recommendation | Main action points | Suggested period in source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Capacity-building programs for stakeholder groups | Programs for forest field staff, Panchayat functionaries, wild gatherers, traders, and Customs officials; develop standard training modules | 2017-2022; 2017-2019 |
| B | Illustrated Compendium of 1,178 traded medicinal plants | Identify organization/core group; prepare and review manuscript; print at least 2,000 copies | 2017-18; 2019-20 |
| C | Posters/brochures on harvesting and post-harvest handling | Develop national prototype and vernacular versions; distribute through Panchayat level | 2017-18; 2018-19 |

## 12.2.8 Institutional mechanisms

### Source assessment

The medicinal-plant sector is described as institutionally complex, spanning wild collection, cultivation, imports, households, folk practitioners, herbal units, trade, regulation, research, and resource users.

The chapter proposes strengthening **NMPB** as the key organization dealing with the medicinal-plant sector. It recommends regional **Medicinal Plant Technical Support Groups (MP-TSGs)** functioning as Centers of Excellence for NMPB and located within existing organizations/institutions that already have medicinal plants or NTFPs as a core mandate.

The source also notes the absence of an institutional mechanism to collate and use annual-return data submitted by domestic herbal units under **Section 157A of the Drug & Cosmetic Rules, 1945**. A web-based data portal is proposed to collect, verify, analyze, and publish such information at national level.

### Recommended action matrix

| Rec. | Recommendation | Main action points | Suggested period in source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Establish regional MP-TSGs in existing institutions | Prepare Terms of Reference; identify suitable institutions; establish long-term agreements; operate as NMPB Centers of Excellence; use nodes for periodic demand/supply review | 2017; 2017-18; 2018-2027 |
| B | Strengthen NMPB infrastructure and manpower | Develop a 10-year institutional consolidation plan | 2017-18 |
| C | Establish web-based National MedPlant Portal | Identify national nodal agency; build portal; monitor industry submissions; collate, verify, analyze and publish periodic annual bulletins | 2017-18; 2018-2025 |

# Source framing and limitations

1. **Historical scope:** The chapter reflects the assessment period ending in **2014-15** and contains a proposed implementation horizon extending to **2030**.
2. **Recommendations are source recommendations:** This document preserves the study's recommendations and does not imply later government adoption or implementation.
3. **Sector growth figures are estimates:** The chapter presents estimated trade volume, trade value, and domestic-industry turnover, not a current national statistical series.
4. **Regulatory discussion is source-era:** References to the Biological Diversity Act, ITC (HS), transit rules, Section 157A, and other policy mechanisms describe the report's assessment and recommendations at that time; they should not be treated as current legal advice.
5. **Trade-entity ambiguity is itself a study finding:** The chapter emphasizes that multiple trade names, botanical equivalents, uncertain source attribution, and inadequate traceability complicate species-level management.
