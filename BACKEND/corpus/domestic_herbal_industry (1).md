## RAG Chunk 001: Executive overview
**Source pages:** 19-20  
**Keywords:** licensed herbal units; sampling; total consumption; plant diversity; turnover

The study states that India's domestic herbal industry consisted of 8,610 licensed herbal units producing healthcare formulations under different Indian Systems of Medicine. Units were categorized as large, medium, small, and very small. Because complete annual-turnover data were unavailable, the study correlated annual turnover with annual consumption of herbal raw drugs, including spices, and used consumption volume as a practical basis for categorization and sampling.
A stratified sample of 692 units was used to estimate national consumption. The study reports active use of herbal materials corresponding to 907 medicinal plant species and estimates total annual consumption at 1,95,000 MT for 2014-15. More than two-thirds of that estimated consumption was attributed to large and medium units. The study also reports that nearly 85% of medicinal plant species used in classical formulations under codified Indian medical streams were still sourced from the wild.
The study estimates total domestic herbal-industry turnover at about Rs. 20,000 crore for 2014-15.

## RAG Chunk 002: Industry distribution by medical system and state
**Source pages:** 20-21  
**Keywords:** Ayurveda; Unani; Siddha; Homeopathy; state distribution; Kerala; Uttar Pradesh; Tamil Nadu

The report states that 87% of licensed herbal units were registered under Ayurveda, 4.9% under Unani, 3.8% under Siddha, and 4.3% under Homeopathy. No licensed unit under the Sowa-Rigpa (Tibetan) stream was reported in the dataset.
Uttar Pradesh had the highest total number of licensed herbal units at 2,247, followed by Kerala at 905. The report notes that 324 of the 328 Siddha units were located in Tamil Nadu; 237 of the 421 Unani units were in Uttar Pradesh and 106 in Andhra Pradesh. Uttar Pradesh also accounted for more than one-fourth of the Ayurveda units. Kerala was reported to have 12% of the country's Ayurvedic herbal units.
The north-eastern states named in the report as having no licensed herbal units were Manipur, Mizoram, Nagaland, Tripura, and Arunachal Pradesh.

## RAG Chunk 003: Unit-size classification and consumption-based categorization
**Source pages:** 22  
**Keywords:** large units; medium units; small units; very small units; annual turnover; MT thresholds

The report describes the Department of AYUSH's four broad size classes: Large (annual turnover > Rs. 50 crore), Medium (Rs. 5-50 crore), Small (Rs. 1-5 crore), and Very Small (< Rs. 1 crore).
For the study, annual raw-drug consumption was used as a proxy for unit size because annual turnover was not available for all licensed units. The study assumes that herbal raw drugs including spices account for approximately one-tenth of unit turnover. For ease of calculation, the average raw-drug price was rounded to Rs. 100/kg. Under that relationship, Rs. 1 crore annual turnover corresponds to about 10 MT of raw-drug consumption, and Rs. 50 crore corresponds to about 500 MT.
The resulting consumption-based thresholds were: Large >500 MT/year, Medium 50-500 MT/year, Small 10-50 MT/year, and Very Small <10 MT/year.

## RAG Chunk 004: Data collection and sampling design
**Source pages:** 22-23  
**Keywords:** Section 157A; Drug and Cosmetics Act 1945; Drug and Cosmetics Rules 1945; sampling; 692 units

Consumption data were obtained from (a) formats submitted by licensed herbal units under Section 157(A) of the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1945 to State Drug Controllers and the National Medicinal Plants Board (NMPB), and (b) direct visits to herbal units and discussions with unit managers and staff.
The report notes that many units had begun maintaining annual raw-drug consumption records in the format prescribed under the 09 July 2008 amendment to the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, 1945. Section 157A required licensed manufacturing units of Ayurveda, Siddha, or Unani drugs to maintain records of raw materials used during the preceding financial year.
The proposed sample was 396 units. More than 700 units ultimately supplied consumption data; after scrutiny for inconsistencies, data from 692 units were considered fit for compilation and analysis. The visits also supported assessment of procurement, storage, use, availability, and nomenclature of herbal raw drugs.

## RAG Chunk 005: Estimated national consumption and high-trade species
**Source pages:** 24-29  
**Keywords:** 1,95,000 MT; 907 species; 198 species; >100 MT/year; category A/B; category C/D

Using the 692 sampled units and extrapolating category-wise to all 8,610 registered domestic herbal units, the study estimates total herbal raw-drug consumption of 1,95,000 MT for 2014-15.
The estimate covers material corresponding to 907 medicinal plant species. The study explicitly excludes entities commonly used as cereals, vegetables, and fruits from the medicinal-plant inventory, although herbal commodities normally traded as spices and forming part of classical formulations were included.
Of the 907 species, 198 were recorded at annual consumption above 100 MT. These 198 species accounted for about 95% of the industry's estimated herbal raw-drug consumption. The report contrasts this with an earlier Ved and Goraya (2008) estimate of 117 species accounting for about 80% of consumption in 2005-06.
The report states that large and medium industries (Categories A and B), representing fewer than 3% of licensed units, consumed more than two-thirds of the total raw drugs, while the 97% of units in Categories C and D consumed the remaining approximately one-third.

## RAG Chunk 010: Statistical profile of consumption by unit category and estimated turnover
**Source pages:** 30  
**Keywords:** mean consumption; standard error; coefficient of variation; confidence interval; turnover

Table 3.5 reports mean annual raw-drug consumption per unit for 2014-15. Large units (Category A) had a mean of 2,203.45 MT/unit/year; medium units (B) 127.37 MT; small units (C) 18.49 MT; and very small units (D) 2.03 MT.
The report notes that the very-small category threshold is below 10 MT/year and interprets the mean of 2.03 MT as indicating a low level of operation. It contrasts this with the large-category mean of 2,203.45 MT, well above the 500 MT consumption threshold.
The coefficient-of-variation values are reported as 12.06 for large, 5.19 for medium, 4.65 for small, and 4.93 for very small units. The report attributes the larger variation among large units to product specificity, especially where patent and proprietary wellness formulations use smaller numbers of constituent raw drugs.
Using an industry-gate average price of Rs. 100/kg for all herbal raw drugs including spices, the report calculates an estimated raw-material value of about Rs. 1,950 crore for 1,95,000 MT and an implied industry turnover of about Rs. 19,500 crore, rounded by the report to Rs. 20,000 crore.

## RAG Chunk 011: Trends: growth of the domestic herbal industry and concentration of consumption
**Source pages:** 30-31  
**Keywords:** growth; turnover; large and medium industries; Dabur; Patanjali; proprietary formulations

The study compares its 2014-15 estimate with Ved and Goraya (2008). It reports approximately 10% higher raw-drug consumption than in 2005-06, while estimated industry turnover increased from Rs. 8,800 crore in 2005-06 to about Rs. 20,000 crore in 2014-15, described in the report as roughly 10% annual growth.
The report associates much of the sector's observed growth with large and medium units. Their share of total raw-drug consumption is reported as more than two-thirds in 2014-15, compared with about 35% in the earlier 2005-06 estimate.
The report names Dabur India Ltd. as a major domestic force and states that Patanjali Ayurved had emerged as a major consumer of herbal raw drugs for diversified fast-moving consumer and wellness products. These statements are descriptions contained in the source study.
The study also records increased manufacture of patent and proprietary wellness formulations alongside classical formulations. Examples mentioned include products marketed around diabetes, joint pain, gastric disorders, obesity, kidney stones, sexual disorders, skin care, and hair care.

## RAG Chunk 012: Trends: high-trade species, extracts, and industry modernization
**Source pages:** 31-33  
**Keywords:** Aloe vera; Amla; extracts; Mentha; rice extract; cosmetics; nutraceuticals; quality control; GMP

The source reports that Aloe vera replaced Phyllanthus emblica (Amla) as the top-traded herbal raw-drug entity in the current study. Aloe vera consumption is reported as 15,677 MT in 2014-15 versus 1,621 MT in 2005-06. The report associates this increase with positioning in health, food, and wellness products.
The report notes a small decline in estimated annual consumption of Amla and Harar (Terminalia chebula) compared with the earlier study and suggests that increased use of deseeded material and extracts may explain part of the change.
Use of extracts is described as an emerging trend. Extracts from more than 500 medicinal plant species were reported in extensive use. Mentha extracts (Mentha arvensis / Mentha piperita) were reported at more than 6,000 MT, with about 650 MT of Amla extract used annually. The report records perceived benefits in authenticity assurance and savings in transport and storage because extracts are less bulky than raw materials.
The report separately states that rice (Oryza sativa) extract exceeded 27,000 MT of estimated use in 2014-15, mainly in cosmeceutical production. Cereals, pulses, vegetables, and fruits were outside the medicinal-plant demand-and-supply scope, while spices were included.
The domestic herbal industry is described as undergoing modernization: increased use of electric or gas-based equipment, more limited use of wood and charcoal, quality-control laboratories, quarantined manufacturing and packaging areas, cleaner operations, labelled and systematically stored raw drugs, inspection and cleaning protocols, and greater use of data-maintenance practices under Section 157A.

## RAG Chunk 013: Botanical identity and trade-name correlation issues
**Source pages:** 34  
**Keywords:** Jatamansi; Nardostachys jatamansi; Valeriana jatamansi; Didymocarpus; Bergenia; botanical identity; adulteration; substitutes

The report emphasizes that herbal raw drugs continue to be traded under local or trade names that vary by region, while herbal units may use API or Sanskrit names. The resulting mismatch can make it difficult to correlate a traded material with its exact botanical identity.
For Jatamansi, the industry commonly correlated the traded material with Nardostachys jatamansi. The report questions whether the stated demand could be supplied by the fragmented wild populations of that species and suggests that Valeriana jatamansi may be involved because it is also sometimes known by the same name.
For material known in trade as pashan bhed, the report discusses Didymocarpus pedicellatus versus Bergenia ciliata and states that the material reported by industry as Didymocarpus may more likely pertain to Bergenia ciliata. The report explicitly notes that, because samples were not accessible, the botanical identity remained unconfirmed.
Other unresolved nomenclatural groups mentioned are shankhapushpi, bala, vidari, jivanthi/jivanti, and daruharidra. A field examination at Tanakpur is described in which material called jivanthi in trade was identified as Flickingeria macraei rather than Holostemma ada-kodien.
The report also flags unusually high consumption records for gandhapatra taila (Gaultheria procumbens and G. fragrantissima), kapur (Cinnamomum camphora), and banslochan (Bambusa arundinacea) for which large supply volumes could not be traced to known plant sources; it states that these materials might not be plant products and calls for further investigation.

## RAG Chunk 014: Cottage-scale herbal manufacturers, nomadic vaidyas, and self-help groups
**Source pages:** 34-37  
**Keywords:** cottage scale; local vaidyas; churnas; nomadic vaidyas; SHGs; herbal raw drugs

In addition to 8,610 licensed units, the report describes a large cottage-scale sector including local vaidyas and small entrepreneurs who prepare herbal formulations, including churnas, for wellness-related conditions and sell them through roadside markets, weekly haats, melas, parks, and religious places.
The report describes some mobile entrepreneurs using diesel-powered grinders to powder and package raw drugs in front of buyers. It also describes nomadic vaidyas using temporary tented "pharmacies" and marketing remedies while claiming specialization in chronic illness, sexual disorders, and sexual vigour. The report states that these categories of manufacturers and dispensers were not registered for making herbal medicines.
Self Help Groups (SHGs), promoted by government agencies for rural livelihoods, are described as another consumer group for herbal raw drugs. Many were promoted by State Forest Departments and State Tribal Development Departments, with some initiatives involving the National Medicinal Plants Board. Their products could be finished or semi-finished goods sold through departmental counters or partnerships with other agencies.
The report states that cottage-scale manufacturers often did not share their recipes or detailed raw-drug consumption data. Consequently, although their collective raw-drug consumption was believed to be significant, the study did not consider it feasible to estimate the sector's total annual consumption because the number and scale of such operations were not known.

## RAG Chunk 015: Study limitations
**Source pages:** 37  
**Keywords:** limitations; sampling; extrapolation; nomenclature verification; equivalents; substitutes; adulterants

The study states that its ultimate analysis is based on 692 sampled units, despite efforts to stratify the broader industry. It acknowledges that niche herbal units using specific materials could have been missed. Artemisia annua is given as an example: its use by Ipca Laboratories for extraction of artemisinin was not captured in the main survey and became known during field visits.
The annual quantity of herbal raw-drug consumption reported by responding units was treated as true for extrapolation to the wider sector and for drawing inferences.
The species listing is based on botanical correlations supplied by sampled herbal units and not on 100% verification against physical samples. The report notes that many units permitted field teams to inspect raw-drug storage areas but were reluctant to provide samples.
Field visits to wild harvest areas and herbal mandis revealed species traded as equivalents of officially recognized herbal raw drugs. The main dataset, however, reflects consumption of officially recognized herbal raw drugs. The report notes that commonly used substitutes, equivalents, and adulterants were included separately in the report's Consolidated Inventory of the Medicinal Plants in Commercial Demand (Annexure I).
