# The Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954

## 1. Document overview

The Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954 is a Central Act (Act No. 21 of 1954) that controls the advertisement of drugs in certain cases and prohibits advertisement of remedies alleged to possess magic qualities, for certain purposes. It was substantially amended by the Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Amendment Act, 1963.

- **Type:** Statute (Central Act), with subordinate Rules — the Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Rules, 1955.
- **Field:** Advertising / labelling claims — not product licensing. Product licensing for Ayurvedic medicines sits under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 and Rules, 1945.
- **Extent:** Whole of India (subject to the savings in the Act for Jammu and Kashmir in the original text) and, by the Act's own words, persons domiciled in India who are outside India, in respect of publications made from India.
- **Core idea:** The Act does **not** ban the manufacture or sale of any drug. It bans *advertising* drugs (and magic remedies) for certain purposes, and bans misleading drug advertisements generally.

## 2. Object and scheme

The Act was enacted to curb:

1. Advertisements that push self-diagnosis and self-medication for serious diseases that need timely medical attention.
2. Advertisements of drugs claimed to affect conception, miscarriage, menstrual disorders or sexual capacity.
3. Advertisements of "magic remedies" — talismans, mantras, charms — claimed to cure or mitigate disease.
4. Advertisements that are false or misleading in any material particular about the drug.

The scheme is: **definitions (s.2) → prohibitions (ss.3, 4, 5, 6) → penalties (s.7) → enforcement powers (ss.8, 8A-type provisions, 9, 9A, 10, 10A) → savings and exemptions (ss.14, 15) → rule-making (s.16) → Schedule of diseases.**

## 3. Key definitions (Section 2)

| Term | Statutory meaning (plain-language summary) |
|---|---|
| Advertisement | Includes any notice, circular, label, wrapper or other document, and any announcement made orally or by any means of producing or transmitting light, sound or smoke. Very broad: packaging text, leaflets, hoardings, TV/radio, social media posts, audio-visual announcements and influencer videos can all be "advertisements". |
| Drug | Includes (i) a medicine for internal or external use of human beings or animals; (ii) any substance intended for use in the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment or prevention of disease; (iii) any article (other than food) intended to affect or influence the structure or any organic function of the body; (iv) any article intended for use as a component of any of the above. Ayurvedic medicines squarely fall within this definition. |
| Magic remedy | Includes a talisman, mantra, kavacha and any other charm of any kind alleged to possess miraculous powers for the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment or prevention of disease, or for affecting the structure or organic function of the body. |
| Registered medical practitioner | A person holding a qualifying medical degree or entitled to be registered under a State medical registration law (the definition cross-references the medical registration statutes). |
| Taking part in publication | Includes printing of the advertisement, and publication outside India by or at the instance of a person residing in India. |

**Why this matters for Ayurveda:** Because "drug" includes any substance intended to affect the structure or organic function of the body, an Ayurvedic proprietary medicine, a classical formulation marketed with therapeutic claims, a "rasayana" tonic, or even a herbal ingredient sold with disease claims, can all be treated as a "drug" for the purposes of this Act.

## 4. Section 3 — Prohibition of advertisement of certain drugs for treatment of certain diseases and disorders

Section 3(1) provides that, subject to the Act, **no person shall take any part in the publication of any advertisement referring to any drug in terms which suggest or are calculated to lead to the use of that drug for:**

1. the procurement of miscarriage in women or prevention of conception in women;
2. the maintenance or improvement of the capacity of human beings for sexual pleasure;
3. the correction of menstrual disorder in women;
4. the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment or prevention of **any disease, disorder or condition specified in the Schedule**, or any other disease, disorder or condition specified in the rules made under the Act.

The proviso to section 3 limits the rule-making power: a disease may be added by rules only if it requires timely treatment in consultation with a registered medical practitioner, or for which there are normally no accepted remedies, and only after consultation with the Drugs Technical Advisory Board (constituted under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940) and, where the Central Government thinks fit, persons with special knowledge of Ayurvedic or Unani systems.

## 5. Section 4 — Prohibition of misleading advertisements relating to drugs

Independent of the Schedule, **no person shall take part in the publication of any advertisement relating to a drug if the advertisement contains any matter which:**

- (a) directly or indirectly gives a false impression regarding the true character of the drug;
- (b) makes a false claim for the drug; or
- (c) is otherwise false or misleading in any material particular.

This is the catch-all provision: even where the disease is not in the Schedule, an advertisement that misrepresents the composition, efficacy, "100% cure", "no side effects", "clinically proven" (without proof), or the nature of an Ayurvedic product, is prohibited.

## 6. Section 5 — Magic remedies

No person carrying on or purporting to carry on the profession of administering magic remedies shall take any part in the publication of any advertisement referring to any magic remedy which directly or indirectly claims to be efficacious for any of the purposes specified in section 3 (the four heads listed above, including Schedule diseases).

## 7. Section 6 — Import and export of prohibited advertisements

No person shall import into or export from India any document containing an advertisement of the nature referred to in sections 3, 4 or 5. Such documents are deemed to be goods whose import/export is prohibited under the Sea Customs Act, 1878 (as that Act then stood), and the customs machinery applies. Cross-border e-commerce, export catalogues and imported packaging inserts can be caught.

## 8. Section 7 — Penalty

**Whoever contravenes any provision of the Act or the rules made under it is, on conviction, punishable:**

- **first conviction:** imprisonment which may extend to **six months**, or with fine, or with both;
- **subsequent conviction:** imprisonment which may extend to **one year**, or with fine, or with both.

**Caution on fine amounts:** Many secondary sources quote monetary ceilings (commonly ₹1,000 for a first offence and ₹5,000 for a subsequent offence). The text of section 7 as reproduced in commonly available compilations states simply "with fine". **Do not quote a fine amount without checking the current official text and any applicable amendment.**

Related enforcement provisions (by subject, with section numbers where confidently identified):

- **Section 8 — Powers of entry, search and seizure:** an authorised gazetted officer of the State Government may enter and search premises, seize advertisements that contravene the Act, and seize records; search/seizure follows the Code of Criminal Procedure as if under a warrant, and the officer must inform a Magistrate.
- **Section 9 — Offences by companies:** the person in charge of and responsible to the company for the conduct of the business, and the company, are deemed guilty; directors/managers/secretaries can also be proceeded against where the offence was committed with their consent, connivance or neglect. **In practice, directors and marketing officers of Ayurvedic companies can be personally prosecuted.**
- **Section 9A — Cognizable offence:** offences under the Act are cognizable (police may act without a warrant).
- **Section 10 — Jurisdiction:** no court inferior to a Presidency Magistrate or a Magistrate of the first class may try offences.
- **Section 10A — Forfeiture:** on conviction, the court may order forfeiture of the documents, articles and things in respect of which the contravention occurred (including seized copies).
- **Section 13 — Other laws not affected:** the Act is in addition to, and not in derogation of, other laws (so prosecution under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, the Consumer Protection Act, the Food Safety and Standards Act or the Cable TV rules remains available).

## 9. Savings and exemptions (Sections 14, 15, 16)

**Section 14 — Savings (does not apply to):**

- (a) a signboard or notice displayed by a registered medical practitioner on his premises indicating that treatment for a section 3 disease/Schedule condition is undertaken there;
- (b) any treatise or book dealing with the subject from a bona fide scientific or social standpoint (academic/educational publishing is protected);
- (c) an advertisement relating to any drug sent confidentially, in the manner prescribed, only to a registered medical practitioner;
- (d) an advertisement relating to a drug printed or published by the Government;
- (e) certain pre-1963 sanctioned publications (with a Government power to withdraw the sanction).

**Section 15 — Power to exempt:** the Central Government may, in public interest, direct that sections 3, 4, 5 or 6 shall not apply, or apply with conditions, to specified drugs, classes of drugs or classes of advertisements.

**Section 16 — Rules:** the Central Government may make rules to (a) specify any disease, disorder or condition to which section 3 applies, and (b) prescribe the manner in which advertisements of the section 14(c) confidential category may be sent. Rules must be laid before Parliament.

## 10. The Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Rules, 1955

The Rules made under section 16 operate in two areas:

1. **Specifying additional diseases/conditions** for the purposes of section 3(1)(d) (subject to the statutory safeguards in the proviso to section 3).
2. **Prescribing the manner of confidential circulation** of drug advertisements to registered medical practitioners (the section 14(c) carve-out).

**Retrieval note:** the Rules are short and are read with the Act. When answering queries, always cite the Act's Schedule as the primary list of prohibited-disease claims and treat the Rules as the power to extend that list and to regulate professional/medical communication.

## 11. Amendment context (2020 draft Bill and later reform proposals) — status caution

A draft **Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) (Amendment) Bill, 2020** was circulated for public consultation. Reports of the draft proposed a substantially expanded list of diseases/conditions and significantly higher penalties for repeat offenders (reported figures in commentary include imprisonment up to five years and fines up to ₹50 lakh for repeated violations).

**Status caution:** a draft bill or draft rule is **not law**. It has no force until enacted/notified and published in the Gazette of India. Do not treat the 2020 draft (or any later draft proposal) as the operative text; verify the current Act, Schedule and Rules on the official source before quoting any figure.

## 12. How this Act applies to Ayurvedic medicines

1. **Ayurvedic products are "drugs"** under section 2(b) whenever they are presented as diagnosing, curing, mitigating, treating or preventing disease, or as affecting structure/function.
2. **Classical or proprietary, the advertising rule is the same.** A licensed Ayurvedic proprietary medicine that claims "cures diabetes" or "cures tuberculosis" breaches section 3 even though the product itself is lawfully manufactured and sold under a drug licence.
3. **Indirect and testimonial claims count.** "Sugar-free in 30 days", before/after patient stories, doctor endorsements on social media, WhatsApp forwards, YouTube videos, podcast mentions and influencer posts are advertisements in the broad statutory sense.
4. **Section 4 catches claims outside the Schedule.** Claims of "complete cure", "guaranteed relief", "no side effects", "100% safe", "replacement for allopathic medicines" can be misleading irrespective of the disease list.
5. **Labelling matters.** Because "advertisement" includes a label and wrapper, the outer carton, insert and leaflet are subject to the same prohibitions; labelling must also comply with the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules (including the claims restrictions applicable to ASU drugs and the Schedule of diseases prescribed for drug labels under that regime).
6. **Magic remedy claims.** Claims of miraculous or occult power for a product (mantra/tantra/astrological framing) attract section 5 in addition to sections 3 and 4.
7. **Interlocks with other law:** the same claim may also breach the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 and the Central Consumer Protection Authority's guidelines against misleading advertisements (2022), the Drugs and Cosmetics Act (misbranding/label offences), and, for food products, the FSS (Advertising and Claims) Regulations, 2018.

## 13. What an Ayurvedic product owner must check

- [ ] Is any claim on the pack, insert, website, marketplace listing, social handle or ad video suggesting cure/mitigation of a **Schedule disease** (diabetes, tuberculosis, cancer, heart disease, hypertension, epilepsy, leprosy, obesity, stones, sterility, etc.)? **Remove or rewrite.**
- [ ] Does any claim directly or indirectly give a false impression or make a false/misleading claim about the product's character (composition, purity, clinical proof, "no side effects")? (Section 4.)
- [ ] Is the product (or its marketing) framed as possessing miraculous/magic powers? (Section 5.)
- [ ] Are influencer, testimonial, doctor and "patient story" posts treated as advertisements and reviewed before publication?
- [ ] Do pack labels, leaflets and e-commerce bulletins repeat the same claims (label = advertisement)?
- [ ] Do export catalogues/ad material avoid prohibited claims, given section 6 (import/export of prohibited advertising documents)?
- [ ] Are directors/marketing managers aware of personal liability under section 9 (offences by companies) and that offences are cognizable?
- [ ] Is the referral/medical-professional communication (if any) confined to the confidential channel permitted by sections 14(c) and 16, and to registered medical practitioners?
- [ ] Is any academic/educational publication genuinely a bona fide scientific or social treatise (section 14(b)) rather than a sales document?
- [ ] Has the current Schedule and any rules made under section 3(d) been checked on the official source, rather than relying on this summary?
