# Hague System for the International Registration of Industrial Designs — Geneva Act (1999)

## 1. What this instrument is

The **Hague Agreement Concerning the International Registration of Industrial
Designs** (originally: *International Deposit of Industrial Designs*) was first
concluded at The Hague on **6 November 1925** and is administered by WIPO. The
current system is built on three Acts:

| Act | Signed | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **London Act** | 2 June 1934 | **Frozen since 1 January 2010** — no new accessions; existing members remain bound |
| **Hague Act** | 28 November 1960 | Open for accession; Common Regulations apply |
| **Geneva Act** | **2 July 1999** | Open for accession; the modern route (compatible with examining offices) |

The Geneva Act entered into force on **18 May 2000** (after six ratifications).
The Hague System now covers roughly **100 countries** — including the EU
(registered designs via EUIPO) and OAPI. **Verify current Contracting Parties
and declarations at the source_url before designating.**

**Core idea:** one international application, in one language, with one set of
fees paid in one currency (Swiss francs) to WIPO's International Bureau,
designating one or more Contracting Parties. The international registration
produces **the same effect as a regularly-filed national application** in each
designated Party; each designated office may still **refuse** protection under
its own substantive law.

> The Hague System is **procedural only**: conditions for protection
> (novelty/originality, novelty grace periods, prohibited matter), the refusal
> procedure's substantive grounds, and the rights obtained are governed by the
> **law of each designated Contracting Party**.

## 3. Filing, registration and publication (Articles 3–6, 9–10)

1. **Entitlement (Art. 3):** any person who is a national of a Contracting
   Party, or has a domicile, habitual residence, or real and effective
   industrial or commercial establishment in the territory of a Contracting
   Party.
2. **Route (Art. 4):** file **directly** with the International Bureau (WIPO)
   or **indirectly** through the office of the applicant's Contracting Party
   (that office may charge a transmittal fee; some offices have declared that
   indirect filing is not available).
3. **Contents (Art. 5):** a request for international registration, prescribed
   applicant data, **reproductions** of the design (or specimens where a
   deferred publication is requested for a two-dimensional design), an
   indication of the **product** or products, the list of **designated
   Contracting Parties**, and the prescribed fees. Up to **100 industrial
   designs** may be included in one application, provided they belong to the
   **same class of the Locarno Classification** (verify current limit and
   unity rules at the source_url).
4. **Optional national requirements:** an Examining Office that requires
   creator identity, a brief description and/or a claim in national
   applications may require the same in international applications (Art.
   5(2)) — check declarations before filing.
5. **Priority (Art. 6):** claim Paris Convention priority from an earlier
   design application filed in any Paris/WTO country; the international
   application itself is a regular filing from its filing date.
6. **Filing date and registration (Arts. 9–10):** the date of the
   international registration is the **filing date** of the international
   application; registration occurs immediately on receipt (or on correction);
   the International Bureau then publishes the registration in the
   **International Designs Bulletin**, and publication constitutes sufficient
   publicity in every designated Party.

## 4. Deferment of publication (Article 11)

- The application may request **deferment of publication** (Art. 5(5)).
- The **prescribed period of deferment is up to 30 months**, but a designated
  Party whose national law allows a **shorter** period may declare that period,
  and the **shortest** declared period governs the whole request; a Party whose
  law allows **no** deferment may so declare (in which case the designation is
  affected — see Art. 11(3)).
- The holder may request **earlier publication**, or **special access** by a
  named third party, during the deferment period; renunciation or limitation
  can prevent publication of the affected designs.
- At the end of the deferment the International Bureau publishes on payment of
  fees, and (for specimen filings) the holder must submit reproductions within
  the prescribed period.

> **Note:** WIPO has published amendments to the Regulations under the 1999 Act
> entering into force on **1 July 2026** (including recording of information
> relating to the creator of an industrial design). Check the current
> Regulations text before filing.

## 5. Refusal of protection (Article 12, Rule 18 of the Common Regulations)

- **Right to refuse:** the office of any designated Contracting Party may
  refuse the effects of the international registration, **in whole or in part**,
  where the conditions for grant of protection under that Party's law are not
  met — but **not** on the ground that national form/content requirements
  differ from the Act's requirements.
- **Time limit (Common Regulations, Rule 18(1)):** the prescribed period for
  notifying a refusal is **six months from the date of publication of the
  international registration**. A Contracting Party whose office is an
  **Examining Office** or whose law allows **opposition** may declare that this
  period is replaced by **twelve months** (Article 12(2) read with Rule
  18(1)(b) of the Regulations under the 1999 Act).
- **Form:** the notification must state **all the grounds** for the refusal;
  the International Bureau transmits it to the holder without delay.
- **Remedies:** the holder enjoys the **same remedies** as if the design had
  been the subject of a national application — at least re-examination, review
  or appeal against the refusal (Article 12(3)(b)).
- **Withdrawal:** the office may withdraw a refusal at any time, in whole or in
  part (Article 12(4)); a withdrawn refusal revives the effect of the
  registration in that territory.
- **Effects absent refusal (Article 14(2)):** where no refusal is notified, the
  international registration has the **same effect as a grant of protection**
  under national law from expiry of the refusal period (or from the earlier or
  later date notified in a declaration).

> **There is no "world design right".** A bundle of independent national or
  regional rights arises; a refusal in one designated Party does not affect
  protection elsewhere, and national invalidation (Art. 15) remains possible.

## 6. Term, renewal and fees (Articles 7, 17)

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| Initial term | **5 years** from the date of international registration (Art. 17(1)) |
| Renewal | Additional **5-year** terms (Art. 17(2)); renewal possible for **some** designated Parties and **some** designs only (Art. 17(4)) |
| Minimum total duration (1999 Act) | **15 years** from the date of international registration, provided the registration is renewed (Art. 17(3)(a)) |
| Longer national terms | Where national law grants more than 15 years, that longer term applies to that designation (Art. 17(3)(b)); each Party notifies its maximum duration in a declaration |
| Grace period | Six months after expiry of a five-year term, with surcharge (Regulations) |
| Fees | Basic fee + **designation fee per designated Contracting Party** (or an **individual designation fee** where declared — Art. 7(2)) + published-application fees as applicable; paid in Swiss francs to the International Bureau |
| Renewal fee timing | Unofficial notice of expiry sent ~6 months before the end of each term |

> For comparison: under the **1960 Act** the international registration is
> renewable only **once** for a further 5 years (minimum 10 years), whereas
> the **1999 Act** allows renewal **twice** (minimum 15 years). Many countries
> allow longer national terms (e.g. up to 25 years in some jurisdictions) —
> check each designation's declared maximum.

## 7. India and the Hague System

**India is not a Contracting Party to the Hague System** (not listed among the
members of the 1999 Act or the 1960 Act in WIPO's published member lists as at
the last verification date — **verify current status at wipo.int**).

- Because India is outside the system, an international application **cannot
  designate India**, and Indian applicants **cannot** file an international
  design application under the Hague System. Indian applicants must use the
  **national route** under the **Designs Act, 2000** and the Designs Rules
  (as amended), filing with the Office of the Controller General of Patents,
  Designs and Trade Marks (ipindia.gov.in), claiming Paris priority where
  applicable.
- **India's accession is under active consideration.** A **concept note on a
  proposed amendment to the Designs Act, 2000** published by DPIIT in
  **January 2026** proposes (among other things) accession to the **Geneva Act
  (1999)** of the Hague Agreement and the introduction of a **Chapter on
  international registrations** in the Designs Act, together with a
  **30-month deferment period** (the maximum allowed under the Hague System),
  in order to permit both Indian applicants to file internationally and
  foreign applicants to designate India. **No accession instrument has been
  deposited as at the verification date — re-check status before advising.**
- India has also signed the Final Act of the **Riyadh Design Law Treaty
  (DLT)** (2024), which is a separate, procedural design-law instrument.
- **Indian national design term:** an initial registration term of **ten
  years** from the date of application, extendable by a further **five years**
  on request (Designs Act, 2000, Section 11) — verify the current section text
  and the extension procedure at ipindia.gov.in.

## 8. What this means for an Ayurvedic product owner (device, packaging, dosage form)

1. **Register the *design*, protect the *function* separately.** Product
   shape/configuration, packaging, dispenser design and ornamental labelling
   can be registered designs; functional features belong in a patent
   application (PCT) — designs do not test inventiveness.
2. **Novelty is national.** Each designated office applies its own
   novelty/originality standard and any grace period; publication anywhere
   (including your own launch or catalogue) may destroy novelty in strict
   novelty jurisdictions. Check each country's grace period before public
   disclosure.
3. **Hague is unavailable for India today.** Use the national Designs Act
   route for India; use Hague for foreign markets that are Contracting
   Parties — but only while India remains outside the system (re-verify).
4. **Mind the Locarno class limit.** A single international application may
   combine designs only within one Locarno class, subject to the unity-of-
   design declarations of designated offices (Article 13).
5. **Calendar the refusal window.** Six months (or twelve, if declared) from
   publication — then protection crystallises (Article 14(2)).
6. **Calendar renewals.** Five-year terms; the 15-year minimum under the 1999
   Act assumes you renew every five years; national terms may be longer.
