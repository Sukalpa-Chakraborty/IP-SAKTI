# Herbal Sector in India: Current Scenario

## 1. Document Overview

This is Chapter 1 of a report on the herbal sector in India, focusing on the current scenario of medicinal plants, their demand and supply, trade, and conservation. The chapter is titled "Herbal Sector in India: Current Scenario" and is part of a larger study commissioned by the National Medicinal Plant Board (NMPB) and carried out by the Indian Council of Forestry Research & Education (ICFRE) in partnership with the Foundation for Revitalization of Local Health Traditions (FRLHT). The study reassesses the demand and supply of medicinal plants in India.

The chapter introduces the importance of medicinal plants in traditional health care systems (Ayurveda, Siddha, Unani, Sowa-rigpa), the transformation of traditional practice towards commercial herbal formulations, the global resurgence of interest in herbal wellness, and the need for periodic assessment of demand and supply. It also reviews previous assessments and outlines the present study's objectives, methodology, and expected outcomes.

## 3. Introduction

<!-- chunk_id: herbal-ch1-intro-001 -->

Medicinal plants have been the mainstay of traditional health care practices across all societies for centuries. A significant population in developing countries continues to rely on herbal drugs, which can be gathered from nature almost free. India has strong traditional health care practices represented by classical systems (Ayurveda, Siddha, Unani, Sowa-rigpa) and diverse folk healthcare practices. The commonality is their dependence on raw material from a large diversity of plant species, estimated at about 6,500 species.

The practice of classical health care streams has undergone a major transformation: the individual physician identifying and preparing medicines has been largely supplanted by the pharmaceutical industry. This has necessitated commercial collection of medicinal plants from the wild through millions of herb gatherers, transported to about 8,600 licensed herbal units. This commercial collection has created a thriving medicinal plant-based economy but has put wild populations under stress.

Global interest in herbal health care is undergoing a change. Plant-based traditional health care practices are witnessing a strong global resurgence, despite concerns about safety, efficacy, and quality. This resurgence includes increased use of complementary and alternative medicine in developed countries. Health is increasingly seen as general well-being, and herbs are believed to provide holistic health care.

This renewed global interest has started a race among traditional societies to grab a share of the increasing global trade in herbal raw drugs and finished herbal products. The Global Wellness Institute valued the 2013 global wellness economy at US$3.4 trillion (Bodekar, 2015). Medicinal plants are now sources of new products for well-being, reducing ageing effects, increasing energy, promoting skin health, adding nutrition, and combating stress. Many wellness requirements are met through functional foods.

The transformation of Indian classical health care practices increasingly depends on ready-to-use commercially prepared herbal formulations, and the resurgence of global interest in herbal-based wellness depends on uninterrupted supplies of medicinal plants. While some medicinal plants are cultivated, major diversity continues to be sourced from forests. Increasing harvesting pressure has brought many species under red-listed categories. It is necessary to know annual consumption levels and trends to manage resources for sustainable supplies of authentic and quality botanical raw drugs.

## 4. Previous Assessments of Demand and Supply of Medicinal Plants in India

<!-- chunk_id: herbal-ch1-prev-assess-001 -->

The commercialization of ASU formulations has led to a thriving raw drug trade. Some herbal raw drug mandis (markets) like Khari Baoli in Delhi have operated for over two centuries. However, there has been no national system to maintain records of medicinal plant species diversity or quantities in trade. Estimates of traded herbal raw drugs vary widely: 400 (Anon., 2000), 1,500 (Jain, 1996), >2,000 (Bode & Maarten, 2004), 2,500 (Chauhan, 1999), and 7,500 (Pushpangadan, 1995). None provide a national list.

The first serious national attempt was by NMPB in 2001-02, commissioning CERPA to study annual trade levels of 162 medicinal plant species. EXIM Bank commissioned FRLHT in 2003 to assess export potential, enlisting 1,200 raw drug entities (880 species), including 42 foreign trade species for 2001. CERPA assessed 1999-2000 demand at 2,34,675 MT with trade value of Rs. 1,275.68 crore. EXIM Bank estimated 2001 consumption at 1,28,000 MT with trade value of Rs. 847 crore, projecting 10% annual growth.

Both studies were largely indicative. CERPA relied on selected manufacturing units; EXIM Bank on industry turnover. Other efforts lacked scale. NMPB in 2006-07 commissioned FRLHT for a national study, which enlisted 1,389 botanical entities (960 species) in trade. This led to research on conservation and plantation programs for Guggal, Asoka, and Dashamoola.

The sector is dynamic and intricate; demand and supply need periodic assessment. The need for periodic review is more important due to global interest in herbs for wellness. Complexity remains: chain of custody for many species is unclear; sources of red-listed species unknown; raw material collected under different names, traded under different names, used under classical Sanskritized names, causing botanical identity issues. Many species are traded as imports (e.g., Banslochan Singapuri, Shatavari Nepali) with no corresponding import records. Botanical correlations need refinement. Data on cultivated species and their share in trade is needed. Demand by parts traded needs assessment for conservation. Supply chains for some entities need detailed working. Better understanding of foreign trade botanicals is needed.

Relative opacity at gatherer, trader, and industry levels complicates assessment. Reluctance to share samples makes species-wise assessment challenging. Any study requires understanding these intricacies.

## 5. The Present Study

<!-- chunk_id: herbal-ch1-present-study-001 -->

As the herbal sector grows, there is a need to closely watch annual demand and supply for sustainable resource supply. NMPB assigned this study to ICFRE to re-assess demand and supply, in strategic partnership with FRLHT, which has comprehensive databases and experience.

**Major objective:** Review knowledge on demand and supply, find gaps, and address them through focused field surveys, stakeholder interactions, and analysis.

**Expected Outcomes:**
1. Assessment of current supply of medicinal plant raw materials from:
   - Wild sources and wastelands
   - Cultivation
   - Imports
2. Determination of demand from:
   - Household/traditional healers
   - Trade and industry
   - Exports
3. Review, study, and bring out:
   a. Analysis of regulatory framework for domestic and international trade.
   b. Capturing major stakeholders in supply chain, their strengths/needs.
   c. SWOT analysis of Indian traditional medicine industry and medicinal plants sector.
   d. Assessment of pricing structure including local taxes/VAT/custom duty at various stages for selected species and trend analysis of top 50 species.
   e. Suggesting measures to capture profile of major traders/wholesalers and trading practices in major herbal mandis for quality improvement.
   f. Suggestive approach for rationalization/fine-tuning of HS coding system for herbal raw drugs used in ASU industry.

**Methodology:**
- Herbal raw drug consumption data for 2014-15 based on stratified sampling of 692 domestic herbal units.
- Foreign trade data for 2005-06 to 2014-15 from DGCIS, analyzed.
- Estimate of rural household consumption based on sample survey of 2,450 households across 15 states.
- Highlighted consumption by folk healers and traditional practitioners.
- Visited >40 herbal raw drug mandis for data on diversity, trade chains, and pricing.
- Visited sites for first-hand data on cultivation and wild collection.
- Reviewed legal and policy structure including ITC (HS) Code system.
- Draft report presented at national consultation on 23 March 2016 at Dehradun.
- Final report presented to NMPB committee on 01 September 2016, chaired by Secretary AYUSH.
- Report organized in 12 subject-specific chapters; Chapters 11 and 12 contain synthesis and recommendations.
- Consolidated inventory of 1,178 medicinal plant species recorded in trade appended.

## 7. RAG Q&A Extraction

### Q: What is the main focus of this chapter?
**A:** The current scenario of the herbal sector in India, including the importance of medicinal plants, transformation of traditional practice, global resurgence of herbal wellness, previous assessments of demand and supply, and the present study's objectives and methodology.

### Q: How many medicinal plant species are estimated to be used in India?
**A:** About 6,500 species.

### Q: What is the role of NMPB in this study?
**A:** NMPB commissioned the study to re-assess demand and supply of medicinal plants in India, assigned to ICFRE in partnership with FRLHT.

### Q: What were the findings of previous studies on demand and supply?
**A:** CERPA (2002) assessed 1999-2000 demand at 2,34,675 MT; EXIM Bank (2003) estimated 2001 consumption at 1,28,000 MT; FRLHT (2006-07) enlisted 1,389 botanical entities (960 species) in trade.

### Q: What are the expected outcomes of the present study?
**A:** Assessment of current supply (wild, cultivation, imports), determination of demand (household, trade, exports), review of regulatory framework, stakeholder analysis, SWOT analysis, pricing structure, trading practices, and HS code rationalization.

### Q: What methodology was used?
**A:** Stratified sampling of 692 domestic herbal units, foreign trade data from DGCIS (2005-06 to 2014-15), sample survey of 2,450 rural households across 15 states, visits to >40 herbal mandis, and field visits for cultivation and wild collection data.

### Q: What are the key challenges in assessing demand and supply?
**A:** Opacity at gatherer, trader, and industry levels; reluctance to share samples; unclear chain of custody; multiple names for same species; unrecorded imports; and lack of national records.

### Q: What is the significance of the global wellness economy?
**A:** The Global Wellness Institute valued the 2013 global wellness economy at US$3.4 trillion, indicating a large and growing market for herbal wellness products.
