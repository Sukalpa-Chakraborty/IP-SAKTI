## 2. Novartis AG v Union of India (Supreme Court, 2013)

**Citation:** (2013) 6 SCC 1 (Supreme Court of India; judgment delivered on
4 April 2013).

### Facts

Novartis AG sought a patent in India for **imatinib mesylate in the beta
crystalline form** (the cancer drug Gleevec/Glivec). The patent was refused by
the Controller and by the Intellectual Property Appellate Board (IPAB), and
Novartis challenged the refusal and the constitutionality of **Section 3(d) of
the Patents Act, 1970** (as amended in 2005, with its Explanation on what
counts as "known substance"/"new form").

### Holdings (as reported)

1. **Section 3(d) is constitutional and is not void for vagueness** — it
   excludes mere discoveries of new forms of known substances unless the new
   form exhibits **enhanced efficacy** known to be so.
2. **The beta crystalline form did not satisfy Section 3(d):** the appellant
   did not show **enhanced therapeutic efficacy** over the known substance
   (imatinib mesylate) — the claim was for a new *form*, not a new therapeutic
   invention.
3. **"Efficacy" means therapeutic efficacy** in the context of medicine — a
   higher bioavailability or a better physical property (e.g. flow,
   compressibility) is not, by itself, enhanced efficacy for a drug.
4. The **amended Section 3(d) applies to pending applications** in the manner
   the Court set out (the amendment was upheld; the Court addressed the
   prospective/retrospective debate around the 2005 amendment).
5. The Court read the patentability standard together with the **objective of
   the Act** (prevention of evergreening; Article 21 and public health
   considerations) and TRIPS (Articles 27, 28) — while affirming India's
   TRIPS-compliant position.

### Relevance to Ayurveda

- A **new formulation, salt, polymorph, particle size or extract ratio** of a
  known Ayurvedic ingredient is not patentable unless **unexpected therapeutic
  efficacy** is shown with data — this is the central patentability filter for
  Ayurveda-derived product patents.
- Incremental innovations must be framed around a **demonstrated therapeutic
  effect** (better efficacy, unexpected synergy, defined and reproducible
  clinical outcome), not merely improved stability, solubility or taste.

## 3. Cadila Healthcare Ltd v Cadila Pharmaceuticals Ltd (Supreme Court, 2001)

**Citation:** (2001) 5 SCC 73; also reported as AIR 2001 SC 1952 and in PTC
(as 2001 (2) PTC 541 SC and, in other sources, 2001 (3) PTC 300 SC — the PTC
citation varies between sources and must be checked). Judgment delivered on
**26 March 2001**.

### Facts

Two group companies, both entitled to use the corporate name "Cadila", sold
anti-malarial drugs for falciparum malaria under the marks **FALCIGO**
(Cadila Healthcare) and **FALCITAB** (Cadila Pharmaceuticals). Cadila
Healthcare sought an injunction for passing off; the District Court (Vadodara)
and the High Court refused, and the matter reached the Supreme Court.

### Holdings (as reported)

1. The Supreme Court **did not interfere with the orders of the courts below**
   but laid down a comprehensive set of **factors for judging deceptive
   similarity** (widely applied since):
   - the nature of the marks (word, label or composite);
   - the degree of resemblance, including **phonetic** similarity;
   - the nature of the goods;
   - similarity in the nature, character and performance of the rival goods;
   - the class of purchasers and their education, intelligence and the degree
     of care they exercise;
   - the mode of purchasing or ordering;
   - any other surrounding circumstances.
2. **No single factor is decisive** and the weight of each varies with the
   facts; the court should take a **holistic** view rather than a mechanical
   comparison of differences.
3. **Medicines demand particular care:** a drug is not an ordinary commodity —
   confusion in medicine can have serious consequences, and the argument that
   prescription drugs are sold only to hospitals/clinicians (hence no
   confusion) does **not** by itself exclude the possibility of a mistake.

### Relevance to Ayurveda

- Ayurvedic brand names must be cleared against **phonetic and conceptual**
  similarity with existing marks for the same therapeutic class.
- For classical-formulation names (e.g. *Triphala*, *Ashwagandha* formulations),
  the descriptive/generic component and the distinguishing element must both be
  assessed — the case supports a careful, multi-factor clearance search rather
  than a reliance on visual difference alone.

## 4. Bajaj Auto Ltd v TVS Motor Co (Delhi High Court, 2008) — advisory jurisdiction

**Court and year:** Delhi High Court, 2008. **Citation to be verified**
(commonly cited in patent commentary as a decision on whether a court may
render an **advisory opinion** on patent validity — the reported PTC citation
should be checked against the original before use.)

### Documented holding (as reported in commentary)

Where a patent has been **granted**, a court may decide infringement and
validity in a suit; but courts should not exercise an **advisory jurisdiction**
to pronounce on the validity of a patent that is **yet to be granted** (or where
the request amounts to an advisory opinion). The dispute concerned Bajaj's
DTS-i (digital twin spark plug ignition) patent — **Indian Patent No. 195904** —
and TVS's CC-VTi technology in the TVS Flame motorcycle.

### Later proceedings (documented)

- The **Supreme Court of India** (Civil Appeal No. 6309 of 2009, judgment of
  **16 September 2009**, reported as (2009) 9 SCC 797 — verify the reporter
  citation) directed the **expeditious disposal** of IP infringement suits,
  expressing displeasure at such suits being kept pending for long periods,
  and applied the Order XVII CPC framework to require early disposal.
- Parallel proceedings continued before the Madras High Court, the Bombay High
  Court and the Intellectual Property Appellate Board; **the parties settled
  the entire dispute in October 2019** and withdrew all proceedings (documented
  in stock-exchange filings and press reports).

### Relevance to Ayurveda

- **Threats actions and declaratory suits:** Section 106 of the Patents Act,
  1970 (claim for declaration of non-infringement / groundless threats) was the
  route used in the litigation; a company asserting an Ayurveda-process patent
  must expect a counter-suit if the threat is unfounded.
- **Speed matters:** Indian courts expect IP suits to be expedited — useful for
  injunction strategy.

**Citation to be verified against the original before reliance.**

## 5. Natco Pharma Ltd v Bayer — first compulsory licence in India (2012)

**Forum and date:** The **Controller of Patents**, Mumbai, granted India's
**first compulsory licence** under **Section 84 of the Patents Act, 1970** on
**28 March 2012**, to Natco Pharma Ltd, for the cancer drug **sorafenib
tosylate** (Bayer's Nexavar), subject to a royalty (reported as 6% of net
sales). **Citation to be verified** (compulsory-licence order reference and the
subsequent appellate history should be checked against the original documents).

### Documented basis of the decision (as reported)

The Controller found, on the evidence before him, that the statutory grounds in
Section 84(1) were made out — in particular **reasonable requirements of the
public** (price and supply conditions) and that the patented invention was not
available to the public **at a reasonably affordable price** — and rejected
Bayer's arguments on working of the patent and on the "grant of licence is a
last resort" contention in the manner reported in the order.

### Appellate history

Bayer's challenge was carried to the **Bombay High Court** and thereafter to
the Supreme Court (leave petitions reportedly declined); the appeal decisions
and their citations should be **verified separately** before being quoted.

### Relevance to Ayurveda

- Compulsory licensing is a **lawful, TRIPS-consistent** remedy (TRIPS
  Articles 7, 8, 31; see the TRIPS file in this corpus) where a patented
  Ayurveda-derived pharmaceutical is unaffordable or unavailable.
- It is a **public-authority** tool, not a private defence: a generic
  manufacturer must apply to the Controller (Section 84), while Sections 92
  and 92A provide the government-driven and export routes.

**Citation to be verified against the original before reliance.**

## 6. R.G. Anand v Deluxe Films (Supreme Court, 1978)

**Citation:** (1978) 4 SCC 118 (Supreme Court of India).

### Facts

The plaintiff (writer of a film story/script) alleged that the defendant's
motion picture substantially copied his work; the defendant denied copying,
asserting independent creation from common source material.

### Holdings (as reported)

1. **Copyright protects expression, not the idea.** A common theme, plot or
   idea cannot be monopolised; only the particular expression is protected.
2. The Court set out the practical test for **substantial copying**, in
   substance:
   - whether the defendant has **drawn or copied the main idea** of the
     plaintiff's work with material and substantial variations;
   - whether an **ordinary viewer/reader, on comparing the two works, is
     driven to the conclusion that one has been copied from the other**, and
     whether the defendant's work creates the impression of being a
     reproduction with added bells and whistles;
   - whether there is a **common source** explaining the similarity.
3. On the facts, the Court held that there was **no infringement** — the
   similarities flowed from the shared theme and stock elements.

### Relevance to Ayurveda

- Copyright does not protect a **formula, therapeutic concept or protocol** —
  only the particular written expression (text, arrangement, graphics, code).
- Reverse-engineering a competitor's published treatment protocol is not
  copyright infringement; but copying their **manual, book or software
  verbatim** is.
- For the corpus itself: a structured study summary may summarise and
  paraphrase source material but must not reproduce protected expression
  wholesale.

**Citation to be verified against the original before reliance.**

## 7. Documented traditional-knowledge and prior-art events

These are not court judgments but **recorded patent-office events** that
illustrate how classical Ayurvedic knowledge operates as prior art. Numbers and
dates should be checked against the patent offices' own records.

### 7.1 Turmeric / haldi — USPTO re-examination (1997)

A **US patent** claiming the use of turmeric (*Curcuma longa*) for **willing of
wounds** was challenged before the **USPTO** by India's **Council of Scientific
and Industrial Research (CSIR)** in **1996**, on the ground that the use of
turmeric powder for wounds was **traditional knowledge in India** (documented
practice, prior publication). The USPTO **rejected/cancelled the relevant
claims** in **1997**. *Patent number and the re-examination certificate
reference should be verified at the USPTO before citation.*

**Lesson:** classical use is prior art when it can be evidenced — documented
traditional use defeats novelty anywhere.

### 7.2 Neem — EPO opposition, EP 0 436 257 revoked (2005)

A **European patent (EP 0 436 257)** on a **method of controlling fungal
diseases on plants using neem (Azadirachta indica) seed extract/oil** was
opposed (including by Indian and environmental bodies) before the **European
Patent Office**. The **EPO Technical Board of Appeal revoked the patent on
24 March 2005**, accepting that the claimed invention lacked novelty/inventive
step over prior art including documented and traditional uses. *The decision
number and the parties should be verified at the EPO before citation.*

**Lesson:** bio-prospecting patents on well-documented traditional plant uses
are vulnerable to opposition — and India's own corpus (Ayurvedic texts, the
TKDL) is exactly the kind of prior art that defeats them.

### 7.3 Basmati — RiceTec US patents (1997–2001)

**RiceTec Inc.** obtained **US patents** (1997) claiming **basmati-like rice
lines, plants and methods**; the claims were challenged on the basis of prior
art including long-standing Indian/Pakistani cultivation of basmati rice. In
the **re-examination/inter partes process**, the **USPTO rejected the majority
of the claims (2000–2001)**, and RiceTec abandoned or lost most of them;
subsequent legislation and India's GI regime addressed the wider branding
issue. *Patent numbers and dates must be verified at the USPTO before
citation.*

**Lesson:** claims over a traditional crop variety are defeasible where prior
art and traditional use are documented; a **geographical indication** is often
the better instrument for such knowledge (see 7.4).

### 7.4 Darjeeling Tea — first geographical indication in India (2000)

**Darjeeling Tea** became the **first product to receive a geographical
indication certificate in India** under the Geographical Indications of Goods
(Registration and Protection) Act, 1999, registered in **2000** in favour of
the Darjeeling tea growers' association (application number and exact date of
registration to be verified against the GI Registry records at
ipindia.gov.in).

**Lesson:** for region-specific Ayurvedic raw drugs with a proven reputation
(for example a single-origin *guggulu*, *ashwagandha* or *pippali* source),
**GI is the correct collective instrument** — but it does not confer exclusive
rights on any single trader, and misuse of the mark can be resisted under
Section 22 of the GI Act.

**Citation to be verified against the original before reliance.**

## 10. How to verify a citation in this corpus

1. **Supreme Court of India** — judgments section at supremecourt.gov.in
   (cause title, diary number and SCC/ AIR citation).
2. **High Courts** — Delhi High Court (delhihighcourt.nic.in), Bombay High
   Court and others' judgment portals; verify the year and the PTC/ AIR
   citation.
3. **Free full text** — indiankanoon.org (search by case name; copy the
   cause title, court, date and judge).
4. **Patent office decisions** — ipindia.gov.in (Controller and IPAB/Appellate
   decisions), uspto.gov, epo.org for the foreign events.
5. **Never quote a citation you have not read** — reporter numbers are the
   single most commonly corrupted field in secondary sources (note the
   conflicting PTC citations for Cadila above).
