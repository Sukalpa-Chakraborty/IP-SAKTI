## Core Concepts

### Intellectual Property

Intellectual property is property created by human intellect in fields such as science, technology, literature, art, trade, and design. Unlike land, buildings, vehicles, or other tangible property, IP is intangible.

### Intellectual Property Rights

IPR are legally recognized privileges granted to creators, inventors, or lawful right-holders. They generally allow the right-holder to use, license, sell, distribute, or restrict unauthorized use of the protected creation or invention.

The legal right applies to the intellectual creation, not necessarily to the physical object in which the creation is embodied. For example, copyright in a book protects the creative expression, not ownership of every physical copy of the book.

### Two broad branches

1. **Copyright and related rights:** Literary, artistic, musical, dramatic, cinematographic, software, database, and performance-related works.
2. **Industrial property:** Patents, trademarks, trade secrets, industrial designs, and geographical indications. Other specialized regimes cover plant varieties, semiconductor layout designs, traditional knowledge, and biodiversity.

## Why IP Matters

IP protection can:

- Incentivize creativity and innovation.
- Encourage public disclosure of inventions in exchange for time-limited exclusive rights.
- Support commercialization, licensing, investment, and technology transfer.
- Help organizations protect reputation, goodwill, research outputs, and market position.
- Contribute to economic, cultural, scientific, and social development.
- Help prevent unauthorized copying and unfair commercial exploitation.

IP protection must be balanced against public interest. Excessively rigid protection can increase costs or restrict access to seeds, medicines, educational materials, and technologies. Indian law therefore contains exceptions, limitations, compulsory licensing mechanisms, farmers’ rights, and public-interest safeguards.

## Patents

### Definition

A patent is an exclusive legal right granted for an invention that provides a new way of doing something or a new technical solution to a problem. The applicant must disclose the invention clearly enough for a skilled person in the relevant field to reproduce it.

### Patentability requirements in India

A claimed product or process generally needs to satisfy:

1. **Novelty:** It must not already form part of the state of the art or public knowledge.
2. **Inventive step:** It must involve technical advancement or economic significance and must not be obvious to a person skilled in the art.
3. **Industrial applicability:** It must be capable of being made or used in industry.

### Patent or trade secret?

- Prefer a **patent** where reverse engineering is likely, the invention has a relatively short commercial life, or public disclosure is unavoidable.
- Consider a **trade secret** where secrecy can realistically be maintained for a long period and reverse engineering is unlikely.
- A patent requires disclosure and provides time-limited territorial rights. A trade secret can potentially last indefinitely but is lost if secrecy is not maintained.

### Patent rights and enforcement

A patent holder can generally prevent unauthorized commercial making, using, selling, offering for sale, or importing of the patented invention, subject to statutory exceptions. Rights are territorial and are commonly described as exclusionary or negative rights.

Possible remedies include:

- Interim or interlocutory injunction.
- Permanent injunction.
- Damages or an account of profits.

The government may use patented inventions in specified circumstances such as national emergency or extreme urgency, subject to applicable legal requirements.

### Examples of eligible inventions

Patents may cover inventions across technological fields, from improved everyday products to biotechnology, pharmaceuticals, electronics, and nanotechnology. Incremental improvements may qualify if they satisfy patentability requirements.

### Non-patentable subject matter discussed

The book identifies exclusions including:

- Inventions contrary to public morality or public order.
- Mere discoveries of existing natural phenomena or substances.
- Mere discovery of a new form or use of a known substance without the required technical contribution.
- Frivolous inventions.
- Mere arrangement or rearrangement of known devices.
- Certain inventions related to atomic energy.
- Literary, dramatic, musical, artistic works, mathematical methods, teaching methods, and similar subject matter protected, if at all, under other laws.
- Semiconductor layout designs, which are separately protected.
- Plants and animals in whole or in part, seeds, varieties, species, and essentially biological processes, subject to statutory exceptions.
- Traditional knowledge and aggregations or duplications of traditionally known properties.

### Patent infringement

- **Direct infringement:** Unauthorized use of a product or process falling within the protected claims, or substantially reproducing the patented invention.
- **Indirect infringement:** Assistance, inducement, or conduct contributing to infringement; the exact legal treatment depends on applicable law and facts.

### Avoiding premature disclosure

Public disclosure can destroy novelty. Before filing:

- Keep technical details confidential.
- Use a non-disclosure agreement when disclosure to investors, partners, or collaborators is necessary.
- File an appropriate application before publication, presentation, sale, or public demonstration.
- Check whether a statutory grace period applies; do not assume it will preserve all rights in every country.

### Indian patent workflow

1. Conduct a prior-art search.
2. Decide whether to file a provisional or complete application.
3. Prepare and file the required forms and specification.
4. Identify the correct Indian Patent Office jurisdiction.
5. Wait for publication, ordinarily after the prescribed confidentiality period.
6. Address any pre-grant opposition.
7. File a request for examination.
8. Respond to examination objections.
9. Obtain grant if all statutory requirements are satisfied.
10. Pay renewal fees and maintain the patent.
11. Monitor infringement and consider licensing or commercialization.
12. Be aware of post-grant opposition and revocation risks.

### Prior-art search

Prior art includes information made available to the public before the relevant filing or priority date. Search sources may include:

- Indian Patent Advanced Search System (InPASS).
- WIPO PATENTSCOPE.
- Espacenet.
- USPTO databases.
- Google Patents.
- Orbit Intelligence and Derwent Innovation.
- Scholarly literature, dissertations, reports, standards, conference proceedings, books, industry publications, websites, technical blogs, and public disclosures.

Search objectives may include novelty, patentability, freedom to operate, validity, infringement risk, and technology landscaping.

### Provisional versus complete application

A provisional application can secure a priority date while development or experimentation continues. A complete specification must generally be filed within the statutory period after a provisional filing. A complete application contains the technical description, claims, and abstract.

### Claims

Claims define the legal boundary of the invention and determine what protection is sought. They must be drafted precisely. The book distinguishes:

- **Independent claims:** Stand-alone claims.
- **Dependent claims:** Claims that incorporate and further limit an earlier claim.

### Publication, examination, and opposition

- Patent applications are ordinarily kept confidential for a prescribed period and then published.
- Third parties may oppose before grant within the statutory window.
- Examination is not necessarily automatic; the applicant must submit the prescribed request for examination.
- A granted patent may be challenged after grant on grounds such as lack of novelty, obviousness, wrongful obtaining, non-patentability, or inadequate disclosure.

### Patent term and maintenance

The book describes a generally twenty-year patent term from the filing date, subject to applicable law and maintenance requirements. Renewal fees must be paid according to the prescribed schedule. Failure to pay may cause lapse, subject to restoration provisions.

### Commercialization and licensing

- **Exclusive licence:** Rights are granted to one licensee for a defined scope or period.
- **Non-exclusive licence:** Rights may be granted to multiple licensees.
- A patent may be licensed where the owner lacks manufacturing capacity, market access, capital, or interest in direct exploitation.
- Compulsory licensing may be available where public requirements are unmet, the invention is not available at a reasonably affordable price, or the invention is not worked in India, subject to statutory conditions.

### International patent protection

There is no universal or world patent. Patent rights are territorial. Applicants may use regional systems or the Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT) to streamline international filing, but protection ultimately depends on national or regional examination and grant.

Indian residents may have restrictions on filing abroad first and may require foreign-filing permission in specified cases. The exact position must be checked under the current Patents Act and rules.

### Utility models

Utility models, petty patents, or innovation patents protect certain incremental technical improvements under less rigorous requirements in some countries. India does not generally recognize a separate utility-model patent system as described in the book.

## Copyright and Related Rights

### Protected subject matter

Copyright concerns original expression, including:

- Literary works.
- Artistic works.
- Musical works.
- Dramatic works.
- Films and sound recordings.
- Computer programs and databases.
- Performances and broadcasts under related-rights regimes.

### Copyright principles

- Copyright protects expression rather than an abstract idea.
- Originality is required to the extent prescribed by law.
- Copyright generally arises automatically, while registration can provide evidentiary advantages.
- Ownership may belong to the author, employer, commissioning party, assignee, or another person depending on the facts and statute.
- Moral and economic rights may coexist.

### Infringement and exceptions

Unauthorized reproduction, communication, distribution, adaptation, performance, or commercial exploitation may constitute infringement. Exceptions include legally recognized fair dealing or fair use situations, education-related uses, religious ceremonies, accessibility exceptions, and other statutory limitations.

### Copyright and the internet

Digital copying, online distribution, technological protection measures, rights-management information, intermediary responsibility, statutory licensing, and access for persons with disabilities are significant issues.

### Copyright duration and registration

The duration varies by category and statutory rule. Registration is not always a condition for subsistence, but creators may register works and retain evidence of creation and ownership. Current law should be checked for the applicable term.

### International copyright instruments

The book discusses the Berne Convention, Universal Copyright Convention, Rome Convention, WIPO Copyright Treaty, WIPO Performances and Phonograms Treaty, and TRIPS.

## Trademarks

A trademark distinguishes the goods or services of one undertaking from those of others. It may include words, names, logos, devices, combinations, shapes, packaging, and in appropriate cases non-traditional signs such as sound or smell, subject to registrability requirements.

### Key points

- Registration is not always compulsory to establish every form of goodwill-based protection, but registration substantially strengthens statutory rights.
- Marks must be distinctive and should not be deceptive, prohibited, or confusingly similar to existing marks.
- Trademark classes are based on the nature of goods and services.
- Symbols commonly include `TM` for a claimed mark and `®` for a registered mark, subject to lawful use.
- Applications are filed and examined through the Trademark Registry.
- Opposition, correction, rectification, removal, renewal, and international filing are addressed under the applicable framework.
- The book discusses the Coca-Cola Company v. Bisleri International matter as an illustrative case.

## Industrial Designs

Industrial design protection concerns the visual appearance of an article, including shape, configuration, pattern, ornamentation, or composition of lines or colours, judged by the eye. It generally does not protect purely functional features.

### Design protection workflow

1. Search for prior designs.
2. Confirm novelty and other eligibility criteria.
3. Select the appropriate classification.
4. File the registration application and representations.
5. Respond to objections.
6. Obtain registration.
7. Monitor unauthorized copying and enforce rights.

The book discusses the Designs Act, 2000, design duration, cancellation, registration forms, international treaties, registration trends, and Apple Inc. v. Samsung Electronics Co. as a significant case.

## Geographical Indications

A geographical indication identifies goods originating in a territory, region, or locality where a quality, reputation, or other characteristic is essentially attributable to that origin.

### Key points

- GI rights are generally collective and linked to eligible producers or authorized users.
- GI protection can support rural livelihoods, cultural heritage, quality assurance, and market differentiation.
- Generic, misleading, prohibited, or otherwise non-registerable indications are excluded.
- Certification and collective marks may complement GI protection.
- Registration requires evidence concerning origin, reputation, quality, production methods, producer groups, and inspection arrangements.
- The book discusses generic and homonymous GIs, appellations of origin, the relationship between GI, trademark, and traditional knowledge, international agreements, and the Banglar Rasogolla v. Odisha Rasogola dispute.

## Trade Secrets

A trade secret is confidential information that has commercial value because it is secret and is subject to reasonable measures to maintain secrecy.

### Examples and protection measures

Examples include formulas, algorithms, manufacturing methods, customer information, business strategies, and technical know-how. Protection measures include:

- Confidentiality and non-disclosure agreements.
- Access controls and need-to-know policies.
- Secure information systems.
- Employee and contractor obligations.
- Document marking and data classification.
- Exit procedures and return or deletion of confidential materials.

India does not have one comprehensive standalone trade-secrets statute as described in the book. Protection may arise through contract, equity, copyright, and breach-of-confidence actions.

### Trade secret versus patent

| Factor | Trade secret | Patent |
|---|---|---|
| Disclosure | Kept confidential | Public disclosure required |
| Duration | Potentially indefinite while secret | Limited statutory term |
| Reverse engineering | May defeat protection | Does not by itself defeat a valid patent |
| Registration | Usually no registration | Formal application and examination |
| Territoriality | Depends on confidentiality and applicable law | Territorial grant |
| Best suited for | Information difficult to reverse engineer | Inventions likely to be reverse engineered or needing clear exclusionary rights |

The book gives Coca-Cola, Google’s algorithm, WD-40, and KFC as examples of well-known trade secrets.

## Traditional Knowledge

Traditional knowledge is community-held knowledge, practices, skills, and innovations transmitted across generations. It may concern medicinal plants, health practices, agriculture, crafts, hunting, food, dance, music, folklore, rituals, and other cultural expressions.

### Main concerns

- Preventing unauthorized patenting or commercial appropriation.
- Recognizing community contributions and benefit sharing.
- Preserving cultural identity and biodiversity.
- Creating prior-art evidence for patent examination.

The Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL) is described as a database documenting knowledge from Indian systems of medicine to help prevent erroneous or exploitative patent claims.

### Patent Cooperation Treaty workflow

1. File an international application through an eligible receiving office.
2. Obtain an international filing date and search report.
3. Consider international preliminary examination where appropriate.
4. Decide which national or regional phases to enter.
5. Meet the separate requirements and fees of each selected jurisdiction.

A PCT application does not itself grant a worldwide patent.

## Retrieval-Oriented Knowledge Units

### Patentability checklist

- What is the invention?
- Is it new compared with public prior art?
- Does it involve an inventive step?
- Is it industrially applicable?
- Is the subject matter excluded?
- Has it been disclosed publicly?
- Is biological material involved, and has source or geographical origin been disclosed?
- Is the correct applicant and inventor information available?
- Is a provisional or complete application appropriate?

### Patent filing checklist

- Conduct prior-art searching.
- Prepare a clear title and technical description.
- Define embodiments and examples.
- Draft independent and dependent claims.
- Prepare the abstract and drawings where needed.
- Complete Form 1 and Form 2 and other applicable forms.
- File with the correct office or online system.
- Track publication, examination, objections, oppositions, and deadlines.
- Pay renewal fees after grant.

### Trademark checklist

- Identify the mark and owner.
- Identify goods and services.
- Search conflicting marks.
- Check distinctiveness and prohibited matter.
- File in appropriate classes.
- Monitor examination and opposition.
- Renew and enforce the registration.

### Traditional-knowledge and Ayurveda-oriented checklist

- Record the community, source, lineage, and context of the knowledge.
- Distinguish documented prior art from a genuinely new formulation or process.
- Check whether the knowledge is already in TKDL or other prior-art sources.
- Obtain permissions and follow access-and-benefit-sharing requirements for biological resources.
- Avoid disclosing confidential community knowledge without appropriate consent.
- Consider whether protection is better addressed through patents, trade secrets, copyright, GI, plant-variety law, biodiversity law, or defensive documentation.
