# Madrid Protocol (1989) — Relevant Provisions for International Registration of Marks

## 1. What this instrument is

The **Protocol Relating to the Madrid Agreement Concerning the International
Registration of Marks** ("Madrid Protocol") was concluded at **Madrid on 27
June 1989** and entered into force on **1 April 1995**. It was adopted to make
the Madrid system accessible to countries whose trade mark law (notably the
**"use" requirement** and **examination on relative grounds**) was
incompatible with the Madrid Agreement of 1891. It is administered by the
**International Bureau of WIPO**.

**Key dates for India:**

| Event | Date |
|---|---|
| India's instrument of accession deposited | **8 April 2013** |
| Protocol entered into force for India | **8 July 2013** |

> India's declarations and current membership status should be re-confirmed
> against WIPO's Madrid Member States list and the Indian Trade Marks Journal
> at the source_url before filing.

## 2. What the Madrid system does (and does not do)

- **One application, one language, one fee (in part)** → an **international
  registration** based on a **basic mark** already filed or registered in the
  **office of origin** (in India: the Trade Marks Registry, ipindia.gov.in).
- The international registration **designates** each Contracting Party in which
  protection is sought; each designated office applies its **own national law**
  and may **refuse** protection (Article 5).
- There is **no "world trademark".** Protection is territorial and subject to
  national examination, opposition, and absolute grounds.
- The international registration is **dependent** on the basic mark for the
  first **5 years** (Article 6(3)) — see Section 5 below.

## 4. Articles 2, 3, 4 — Basic mark, international application, national effect

- **Article 2 (Securing protection; office of origin)** — protection is secured
  through an **international registration** administered by the International
  Bureau. The **office of origin** is the office of the Member where the
  applicant has a real and effective industrial or commercial establishment, or
  (failing that) where it is domiciled, or (failing that) where it is a national
  of that Member. The office of origin transmits the international application
  to the International Bureau.
- **Article 3 (International application)** — the international application
  must be on the prescribed form, in one language, and must contain: a request
  for the recording of the mark in the international register; the name and
  address of the holder; a list of the Contracting Parties in which protection
  is sought (and the list of goods/services, with classes); and a reference to
  the **basic application/registration** (filing date and number). Certain
  **exclusions** apply: marks that do not comply with requirements of form,
  and goods/services not covered by the basic mark.
- **Article 4 (Effect of international registration)** — the effect of the
  international registration in each designated Contracting Party is the same
  as if the mark had been **filed directly** with that office **on the date of
  the international registration** (subject to any priority claim and to the
  effects of any refusal under Article 5). The designated office may **not
  invoke the Madrid Agreement's own provisions** where the Contracting Party is
  a Party to the Protocol.
- **Article 3ter (Territorial effect)** — in each designated Contracting
  Party, the international registration shall have the **same effect as a
  national registration**, subject to national law.

**India's treatment:** an international registration designating India is
examined by the Indian Trade Marks Registry under the **Trade Marks Act, 1999
and Trade Marks Rules, 2017 (as amended)** — including absolute grounds
(Section 9), relative grounds (Section 11), opposition (Sections 20–21), and
the specific Indian requirement of a **declaration of intention to use** (see
Section 7).

## 5. Article 5 — Refusal; Article 6 — Validity, dependence and independence

### 5.1 Article 5 (Refusal of Protection)

- Each designated office may **refuse protection** under its national law, by
  means of a **statement of refusal**, communicated to the International
  Bureau through the office of origin.
- Offices may apply **provisional refusals** (e.g. subject to opposition
  outcomes), and must state the **grounds** for refusal.
- Time limits for issuing a refusal (and for requesting substantive
  examination) are governed by the Protocol/Regulations — **India declared an
  **18-month** refusal period** under **Article 5(2)(b)** (see Section 7).
- Where a refusal is issued, the applicant may file a **response** through the
  office of origin, and may pursue **appeals/opposition** before the national
  office/courts.
- **Cancellation/invalidation** after grant follows national law.

### 5.2 Article 6 (Period of Validity; Dependence and Independence)

- The international registration is valid for **10 years** from the date of
  the international registration, **renewable** under Article 7 (each renewal
  for 10 years, renewable indefinitely).
- **Dependence (Article 6(3)):** for **5 years** from the date of the
  international registration, the international registration is **dependent on
  the basic mark** — if the basic mark is cancelled, refused or withdrawn
  (wholly or in part) in the office of origin before the expiry of 5 years, the
  international registration shall be cancelled or restricted accordingly.
- **Independence (Article 6(4)):** after the expiry of the 5-year period, the
  international registration becomes **independent** of the basic mark (and of
  any renewal thereof).

> **Practical consequence:** keep the basic Indian application/registration
> alive for at least **5 years**; a lapse or abandonment of the basic mark in
> the first 5 years can trigger cancellation of the international registration
> for the designated countries.

### 5.3 Article 7 (Renewal) and Article 8 (Fees)

- **Article 7** — renewal is requested from the International Bureau, with the
  prescribed fees, before expiry; there is a **grace period** (6 months, with
  surcharge) under the Regulations.
- **Article 8** — the fee structure comprises a **basic fee**, a
  **complementary fee** for each designated Contracting Party (unless the
  Contracting Party has declared a refusal of the complementary fee and uses a
  different structure), and a **supplementary fee** for each class of goods or
  services beyond the first three (in the standard schedule). India declared
  under **Article 8(7)(a)** that it applies an **individual fee** — see
  Section 7.

## 6. Article 9, 9bis, 9quinquies — Ownership change and transformation

- **Article 9 (Recordal of change in ownership)** — the change of ownership of
  an international registration is recorded in the International Register at
  the request of the holder, on payment of the prescribed fee; the recordal has
  **effect in each designated Contracting Party** subject to national law.
  **India declared that recordal of a licence or assignment in the
  International Register has no effect in India** — see Section 7.
- **Article 9bis (Transformation)** — where the international registration is
  cancelled at the request of the office of origin (e.g. because the basic mark
  is cancelled), the holder may request **transformation**: the international
  registration is replaced by a **national application in each designated
  Contracting Party** claiming the priority of the international registration,
  provided the national requirements are met.
- **Article 9quinquies (Transformation of the basic application/registration)** —
  where the **basic application/registration** ceases to have effect, a
  corresponding **transformation** of the international registration may be
  requested.

> Article 9 = ownership recordal; **transformation = Articles 9bis and
> 9quinquies.**

| Topic | India's declared position (verify against WIPO notices) |

## 8. Indian national law that still applies to a Madrid mark designating India

| Issue | Indian law |
|---|---|
| Absolute grounds (descriptiveness, distinctiveness, contrary to public policy) | Trade Marks Act, 1999, **Section 9** |
| Relative grounds (identical/similar marks, prior rights, passing off) | Trade Marks Act, 1999, **Section 11** |
| Opposition | Trade Marks Act, 1999, **Sections 20–21**; Trade Marks Rules, 2017 |
| Use requirement / non-use cancellation | Trade Marks Act, 1999, **Section 47** (registered proprietor's rights; use before registration), **Section 57** (revocation/rectification) |
| Assignment and transmission | Trade Marks Act, 1999, **Sections 40–45** (in India, recordal before the Registry is required for effect) |
| well-known marks / honest concurrent use | Trade Marks Act, 1999, **Sections 11(2), 11(6), 12** |
| Traditional knowledge / TK and GI restrictions | Trade Marks Act, 1999, **Section 34** (marks consisting of matter likely to mislead or that are contrary to law), **Section 23A** (protection of traditional terms, as notified) |
| Ayurvedic/classical-formulation names | Section 9 (descriptiveness/laudatory), Section 11 (prior rights), Section 34 (matter likely to mislead), and consumer-protection law |

## 9. Strategic sequence for an Ayurvedic brand owner

1. **File the basic Indian mark first** (or use a pending Indian application).
   The international application must reference a valid basic mark; and the
   5-year dependence rule (Article 6(3)) means the basic mark must be kept
   alive.
2. **Choose the designations deliberately.** Each designation carries a
   complementary fee (or India's individual fee) and national examination —
   avoid "designate everything".
3. **Budget for national fees and translations.** India's individual fee, and
   opposition/prosecution costs in each designated country, are separate from
   WIPO fees.
4. **Track the refusal window.** India's 18-month refusal period means Indian
   objections arrive later than in many countries; calendar responses.
5. **Record licences/assignments in India.** WIPO recordal has no effect in
   India — file the assignment/licence with the Indian Registry.
6. **Renew at 10 years** (Article 7), and remember the grace period is short.
