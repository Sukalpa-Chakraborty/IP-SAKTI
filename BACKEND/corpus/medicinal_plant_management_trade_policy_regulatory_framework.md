# Medicinal Plant Management and Trade in India: Policy and Regulatory Framework

## RAG Knowledge Document

> **Source chapter:** Chapter 10, *Medicinal Plants: An Assessment of their Demand and Supply in India*
>
> **Scope:** Wild harvest, cultivation, transportation, domestic and foreign trade, conservation, research, and policy/regulatory framework for medicinal plants in India.
>
> **Source period/context:** The chapter describes the legal and administrative framework as discussed in the source report, including rules and policy references current to the report's publication period. Legal provisions are reproduced here as source-derived historical content and should not be treated as current legal advice.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

India's medicinal-plant sector involves nearly **1,200 medicinal plant species** in commercial use for healthcare formulations under the Indian Systems of Medicine. Raw drugs from these plants are collected from the wild, cultivated, or imported, then transported through multiple trade channels to domestic herbal industries, export processors, and retail markets.

The source identifies a major regulatory problem: medicinal plants are governed by multiple central and state-level laws, rules, administrative procedures, and international obligations. These frameworks vary considerably among states and can create delays in interstate movement of herbal raw drugs.

The study reports that:

- Nearly **90% of medicinal plants used by rural households and folk healers**, by both species and quantity consumed, are sourced from the wild.
- For the domestic herbal industry, about **72% of medicinal plant species** and **50% of annual quantities consumed** are sourced from the wild.
- Around **50% of wild collection is destructive**, involving roots, rhizomes, bulbs, whole plants, bark, wood, or similarly destructive plant parts.
- **344 medicinal plant species** had been assessed as facing different categories of threat according to the cited FRLHT database.
- The herbal sector had grown at roughly **10% over the preceding decade** in the source's assessment.

The chapter argues for a **comprehensive national medicinal-plant policy/strategy** integrating conservation, cultivation, trade, quality, market linkages, research, and regulation.

## 2. Core Problem: Fragmented Governance

### 2.1 Regulatory fragmentation

Wild harvest, cultivation, transport, and trade of medicinal plants are governed by several legal and administrative regimes. Many provisions differ between states.

This fragmentation causes:

- Complex compliance requirements.
- Delays in transportation between states.
- Non-uniform rules for the same medicinal plant.
- Difficulties in interstate trade.
- Gaps in data on actual harvesting.
- Different approaches to conservation and research.

### 2.2 Need for a national strategy

The source argues that conservation and research are also weakened by the absence of a comprehensive national policy/strategy. Different states and organizations consequently follow different conservation protocols and research priorities.

The proposed response is a national strategy covering the complete medicinal-plant value chain rather than treating harvest, cultivation, trade, conservation, and research as separate issues.

## 3. Medicinal Plant Supply and Wild Harvest

### 3.1 Commercially used medicinal plants

Nearly **1,200 medicinal plant species** are described as being in commercial use for Indian Systems of Medicine.

The supply chain includes:

1. Wild collection.
2. Cultivation.
3. Importation.
4. Aggregation by traders and local agents.
5. Transportation across states.
6. Processing and value addition.
7. Domestic herbal manufacturing.
8. Export and retail distribution.

### 3.2 Dependence on wild resources

The source emphasizes continued dependence on natural populations:

| User segment | Species sourced from wild | Quantity sourced from wild |
|---|---:|---:|
| Rural households + folk healers/traditional practitioners | ~90% | ~90% |
| Domestic herbal industry | ~72% | ~50% |

The approximately 90% figure for rural households and folk healers is stated as applying to both species and quantities consumed.

### 3.3 Destructive collection

Nearly half of wild collection is described as destructive because harvesting removes plant organs needed for regeneration, including:

- Roots
- Rhizomes
- Bulbs
- Whole plants
- Bark
- Wood

This creates additional pressure on wild populations.

### 3.4 Threat status

The report cites **344 medicinal plant species** as already assessed as facing different threat categories.

The source attributes declines in key species to over-collection driven by domestic and foreign medicinal markets.

### 3.5 Forests as the major wild-harvest source

Forests are identified as the main source of wild medicinal-plant harvest.

In many states, Forest Working Plans prescribe approximately **3-4 year rotational harvesting** for species requiring destructive collection, intended to allow regeneration and population recovery.

For species harvested for fruits or flowers, such as:

- Tamarind
- Mahua
- Sal
- Amla

annual harvesting is permitted according to the source.

### 3.6 Harvest outside forests

Self-grown medicinal plants occurring outside forests are also an important source. These may occur in:

- Agricultural fields
- Fallow land
- Roadsides
- Water bodies
- Wastelands
- Other non-forest landscapes

The source states that harvesting from these areas generally occurs **without a regulatory mechanism**, and no agency maintains systematic records of such harvest.

## 4. Indian Forest Act, 1927

The Indian Forest Act, 1927 is presented as a central legal foundation for forest governance.

### 4.1 Forest notification and authority

The Act enables government notification of forests and gives government authority over forest produce from notified forests.

The source states that approximately **24% of India's geographical area** was notified as forest and under the administrative control of state Forest Departments at the time of the report.

### 4.2 Medicinal plants under the Act

Most medicinal plants are described as falling under **Section 2(4)(b)** and generally not subject to regulation unless extracted from forests.

Some materials, including:

- Kuth
- Myrobalans
- Bark and wood-oil from certain trees

are described as covered under **Section 2(4)(a)**, with subsequent state amendments adding additional medicinal species.

### 4.3 Community collection rights

The Act recognizes certain rights of local communities to collect forest produce for self-use and/or barter, except where species are reserved, nationalized, or prohibited under other laws.

State-level implementation varies significantly.

Examples in the source:

- **Himachal Pradesh:** local wild gatherers may register with the Forest Department and receive permits for collection during specified periods.
- **Uttarakhand:** local Van Panchayats have responsibilities relating to wild harvest by local communities.
- **Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh:** local communities may freely harvest medicinal plants in specified contexts; nationalized species may be collected through JFMCs.
- **Karnataka:** harvesting of nationalized species is conducted through auction of forest blocks.

## 5. Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972

### 5.1 Specified plant protection

The Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 does not specifically focus on medicinal plants but protects specified plant species listed in **Schedule VI**.

The source describes Sections **17A to 17H** as provisions governing protection of specified plants.

### 5.2 Section 17A: prohibited acts

Under the source's presentation of Section 17A, persons are prohibited from willfully:

- Picking specified plants.
- Uprooting specified plants.
- Damaging or destroying specified plants.
- Acquiring or collecting specified plants from protected forest areas.
- Possessing, selling, offering for sale, transferring, or transporting specified plants or their derivatives.

### 5.3 Section 17D: dealer licensing

The source states that dealing in specified plants or their parts/derivatives requires a license from the Chief Wildlife Warden or another authorized state officer.

### 5.4 Medicinal plant example

**Kuth (Saussurea costus / Saussurea lappa)** is identified as an important medicinal plant listed in Schedule VI.

The source also notes that states may add additional species of conservation concern under their own regulatory frameworks.

## 6. Biological Diversity Act, 2002

### 6.1 Main objectives

The Biological Diversity Act, 2002 is described as pursuing three main objectives:

1. Conservation of biodiversity.
2. Sustainable use of biological resources.
3. Equitable benefit sharing from use of biological resources.

### 6.2 Section 38

Section 38 is described as empowering notification of species of conservation concern and regulation or prohibition of their wild collection and trade.

The source reports that **118 plant species** had been notified under Section 38 through state-level notifications covering **17 states** at the time of the report.

### 6.3 Biodiversity Management Committees

The Biological Diversity Rules, 2004 provide for local-level institutions called **Biodiversity Management Committees (BMCs)**.

Their stated functions include promoting:

- Conservation.
- Sustainable use.
- Documentation of biodiversity.
- Habitat conservation.
- Conservation of landraces and folk varieties.
- Documentation of traditional biodiversity knowledge.

### 6.4 People's Biodiversity Registers

A major function of BMCs is preparation of **People's Biodiversity Registers (PBRs)** in consultation with local communities.

The source describes PBRs as records of:

- Local biological resources.
- Availability of those resources.
- Medicinal uses.
- Other uses.
- Associated traditional knowledge.

## 7. Panchayat (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA)

PESA extends the principles of the 73rd Constitutional Amendment to Scheduled Areas and gives statutory recognition to Gram Sabhas and customary practices.

The source describes PESA as creating scope for community-based conservation and recognizing community control over natural resources, including Minor Forest Produce (MFP).

### Key implications described in the source

- Greater local control over natural resources.
- Recognition of indigenous systems and local practices.
- Scope for Gram Sabha control over community resources.
- Potential transfer of ownership rights over MFP to local Gram Sabhas.
- Scope for community-based conservation.

### Implementation limitation

The source argues that implementation is limited because:

- MFP itself remains insufficiently defined under the Act.
- States develop their own MFP lists.
- Panchayats often lack capacity.
- Harvest-data management protocols are weak.
- The institutional system for compiling wild-harvest databases has not been sufficiently developed.

---

## 8. Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006 (FRA)

The Forest Rights Act (FRA) recognizes and vests community tenure and forest-related rights in forest-dwelling Scheduled Tribes and other traditional forest dwellers.

### 8.1 Community forest resources

The source describes community forest resources as common forest land within traditional or customary village boundaries or seasonal landscapes used by pastoral communities, including certain reserved/protected forests and protected areas with traditional community access.

### 8.2 Minor Forest Produce

Section 2(i) is described as defining MFP broadly to include non-timber forest produce of plant origin, including:

- Bamboo
- Brushwood
- Stumps
- Cane
- Tussar cocoons
- Honey
- Wax
- Lac
- Tendu/kendu leaves
- Medicinal plants and herbs
- Roots
- Tubers

### 8.3 Community rights under Section 3

The source identifies rights including:

- Ownership and access to collect, use, and dispose of MFP traditionally collected within or outside village boundaries.
- Local processing and value addition.
- Transport by head-load, bicycle, and handcart for livelihood purposes.
- Rights to protect, regenerate, conserve, or manage community forest resources.
- Access to biodiversity and community intellectual-property/traditional-knowledge rights.

Motor-vehicle transport remains subject to applicable transit rules.

## 9. Implementation Issues in Wild Harvest Regulation

Field visits and stakeholder discussions identified several weaknesses.

### 9.1 Working Plan implementation

Rotational harvesting prescriptions are not fully implemented in many states because of staff constraints.

The source reports clandestine removals from areas officially closed for harvesting. Species identified as suffering from such pressure include:

- *Paris polyphylla*
- *Trillidium govanianum*
- *Fritillaria roylei*

### 9.2 Regulation of Kuth

*Kuth* (*Saussurea costus*) has limited wild populations in Kashmir. Major supplies are reported to come from cultivation in Lahaul, Himachal Pradesh, with some supplies from Uttarakhand.

The source reports declining cultivation due to complex regulatory procedures for trading cultivated material.

It recommends reviewing the regulatory regime to facilitate cultivation of conservation-concern species.

### 9.3 Wild-harvest data gap

After implementation of PESA and FRA, greater responsibility for MFP management has been devolved to Panchayats and traditional forest dwellers.

However, standardized procedures for recording and compiling medicinal-plant harvest data remain insufficiently developed.

The source identifies this as a major national data gap.

## 10. Cultivation of Medicinal Plants

### 10.1 Cultivation-only species

Some medicinal plants used commercially are described as being supplied exclusively through cultivation and integrated into local agricultural systems.

Examples:

- Isabgol
- Tulasi
- Senna
- Mentha

The source states that cultivation of species not found in the wild in India can, under the described framework, be free from certain forest and biodiversity regulations as "normally traded commodities."

### 10.2 Dual wild-and-cultivated species

Other plants occur both in the wild and under cultivation.

Examples include:

- Amla
- Bach
- Basuti
- Musli
- Kuth
- Ashwagandha
- Shatavari

Their supplies can therefore come from both wild collection and cultivation.

### 10.3 Regulatory implication

The source states that cultivation of species also occurring in the wild may attract regulation under forest and biodiversity laws.

### 10.4 Section 17C: cultivation of specified plants

For specified plants listed under Schedule VI of the Wildlife (Protection) Act, such as Kuth, cultivation without a license is prohibited under the source's presentation of **Section 17C**.

A license must specify:

- The cultivation area.
- Applicable cultivation conditions.

### 10.5 State variation

Examples given:

- **Himachal Pradesh:** farmers cultivating certain medicinal plants may need to register with the Forest Department.
- **Madhya Pradesh:** cultivation of medicinal plant species has, in certain cases, been exempted from comparable regulatory mechanisms to facilitate cultivation.

The source notes that notification of conservation-concern species under Section 38 of the Biological Diversity Act can nevertheless introduce stricter trade controls and discourage cultivation.

## 11. Transportation and Domestic Trade Regulation

Medicinal raw drugs obtained from wild resources or cultivation must move from production areas to trade, processing, consumption, and export centers.

The source identifies **Forest Produce Transit Rules** as an important state-level regulatory mechanism.

### 11.1 Forest Produce Transit Rules

These rules generally regulate:

- Storage.
- Movement.
- Interstate transport.
- Export of forest produce from production areas.
- Documentation accompanying consignments.

The source states that consignments commonly require an Export Permit issued by an authorized authority upon payment of a nominal fee, but the exact system varies by state.

### 11.2 State-level differences

Examples provided by the source:

| State | Main framework described in source |
|---|---|
| Himachal Pradesh | Export permits for common species can be issued by Panchayat Pradhans; conservation-concern species handled by DFOs. |
| Madhya Pradesh | Export permits removed for most material except notified species. |
| Other states | DFOs generally continue to issue export permits. |
| West Bengal | Permit within specified areas; certificate of origin required for certain forest produce. |
| Maharashtra | Transit pass required for movement into/from/within districts; no pass within village/town limits. |
| Rajasthan | Forest produce generally requires a pass, subject to stated exemptions. |
| Kerala | Transit pass required; separate procedures for government and private land. |
| Odisha | Transit permits generally unnecessary for minor forest produce within districts, subject to listed exceptions. |
| Himachal Pradesh | Rules distinguish exempted private-land species from species requiring permits; cultivation certification and DFO registration may apply. |
| Arunachal Pradesh | Movement/import/export/collection of forest produce requires a pass under the cited state framework. |
| Andaman & Nicobar Islands | Transit pass applies to forest produce from any source. |

### 11.3 Non-uniformity problem

State rules may treat the same plant differently. The source gives these examples:

- *Taxus wallichiana*: trade prohibited in Himachal Pradesh but allowed in Uttarakhand.
- Kuth: prohibited from wild trade in Jammu & Kashmir, while cultivated Kuth from Lahaul, Himachal Pradesh, is traded under the applicable legal framework.

The source states that non-uniform rules can result in:

- Clandestine removals.
- Informal trade.
- Border delays.
- Additional transaction costs.

It therefore calls for **harmonization of state transit rules**.

## 12. CITES and International Regulation

India is described as a signatory to the **Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES)**.

CITES regulation in India is described as being implemented through a combination of:

- Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972.
- Export and Import Policy / foreign-trade framework.
- Customs Act, 1962.

### 12.1 CITES Appendix I medicinal plants

The source states that six native Indian plant species/species groups under Appendix I are particularly important in the regulatory context:

1. Beddome's cycad — *Cycas beddomei*
2. Blue Vanda — *Vanda coerulea*
3. Kuth — *Saussurea costus*
4. Lady's slipper orchids — *Paphiopedilum* spp.
5. Pitcher plant — *Nepenthes khasiana*
6. Red Vanda — *Renanthera imschootiana*

### 12.2 Appendix I export conditions

For an Appendix I specimen, the source describes the need for an export permit and satisfaction of conditions including:

- Scientific authority advice that export will not be detrimental to survival.
- Confirmation that the specimen was obtained legally.
- Appropriate preparation and shipment conditions for living specimens.
- An import permit from the destination country.

### 12.3 Appendix I import conditions

Import of an Appendix I specimen requires an import permit and an export permit or re-export certificate, subject to the conditions described by the source.

The source includes requirements that the import be non-detrimental to species survival and that living specimens be appropriately housed and cared for.

### 12.4 Appendix I re-export

A re-export certificate is required, with confirmation that the material was imported in accordance with CITES requirements and, where relevant, that living material can be shipped safely.

## 14. Export of Cultivated Material from Protected/Listed Species

The source states that trade in plants and plant parts of species listed under the Wildlife (Protection) Act, CITES Appendix I, or relevant export licensing notes can be allowed when the produce is legally obtained through cultivation and the prescribed documentation is available.

### Key documents described

- Certificate of Legal Possession from the jurisdictional Divisional Forest Officer.
- Certificate of Cultivation from the relevant authority.
- Transit Pass where applicable.
- CITES export permit for species where CITES requirements apply.
- Nursery/site registration for cultivation of certain Appendix I species.

For sites inside forest areas, a transit pass from the relevant DFO may be required. For cultivation outside forests, the source describes certification by district agriculture, horticulture, or forest authorities.

## 15. Value-Added Formulations and Export Exceptions

The source describes special treatment for formulations and value-added products derived from otherwise restricted plants.

It states that certain formulations may be freely exported when plant portions/extracts from prohibited species are present only in **unrecognizable and physically inseparable form**.

The source further describes provisions allowing:

- Value-added formulations using imported prohibited-listed species, subject to affidavit requirements.
- Herbal/Ayurvedic formulations whose labels do not identify ingredients from prohibited plants to be exported without the stated certification requirements.

These provisions are reproduced as **source-era regulatory descriptions** and should not be assumed to represent current law.

## 17. Import of CITES-Listed Medicinal Plants

The source states that imports of CITES-listed medicinal plants, their products, and derivatives are subject to CITES provisions.

### Requirements described

- CITES import permit issued by the Regional Deputy Director (Wildlife).
- CITES export permit issued by the exporting country.
- For seeds intended for planting/sowing, an import permit under the Plants, Fruits and Seeds (Regulation of Import into India) Order, 1989.
- For seeds intended for consumption, an import license as described by the source.

The source states that there was **no general negative list of imports** equivalent to the export negative list.

## 19. ITC (HS) Coding System

India's import/export system uses **Indian Trade Classification (ITC) based on the Harmonized System (HS)** developed under the international customs framework.

The source explains that Indian Customs used an **8-digit ITC (HS) code** system for foreign trade.

### 19.1 Problem with medicinal plants

Medicinal plants do not fit neatly into a single ITC (HS) category.

The largest concentration is under:

- **Chapter 12**
- **Heading 1211**

Some medicinal-plant items also occur under Chapters:

- Chapter 9
- Chapter 13
- Chapter 14

### 19.2 "Others" categories

Many medicinal plants are grouped under broad "Others" codes such as:

- 121190.19
- 121190.29
- 121190.39
- Similar catch-all categories

This prevents reliable plant-species-level analysis.

### 19.3 Data coverage problem

The source states that the 8-digit system captures only a portion of the approximately **400 medicinal plant entities** involved in foreign trade with India.

The result is insufficient species-level data for:

- Resource management.
- Conservation planning.
- Cultivation policy.
- Export/import strategy.
- Monitoring of high-risk species.

### 19.4 Proposed improvement

The source recommends selectively adding **two more digits** to selected ITC (HS) codes, while retaining the fundamental structure of the existing classification system.

## 20. Conservation and Research

### 20.1 Current conservation approach

Many medicinal plants have been assessed as red-listed or included under national/international protection mechanisms.

The source describes the principal immediate response as placing species on negative/prohibited lists.

### 20.2 Medicinal Plant Conservation Areas (MPCAs)

The **Foundation for Revitalisation of Local Health Traditions (FRLHT)** had supported medicinal-plant conservation areas in several states, including:

- Kerala
- Karnataka
- Tamil Nadu
- Andhra Pradesh
- Maharashtra
- Chhattisgarh
- Arunachal Pradesh
- Uttarakhand

The **National Medicinal Plants Board (NMPB)** had also supported MPCAs in additional states.

### 20.3 Lack of national integration

The source identifies a major weakness: conservation initiatives remain state-specific because there is no comprehensive national policy and common framework connecting them.

Some states maintain MPCAs as strict protection areas, while others conduct augmentation planting.

### 20.4 Research fragmentation

Many organizations conduct medicinal-plant research independently.

The source expresses concern about:

- Duplication of research effort.
- Lack of national coordination.
- Highly scattered research priorities.
- Limited ability to generate nationally actionable results.

## 21. Recommended National Policy / Strategy

The chapter concludes that India needs a comprehensive national medicinal-plant policy/strategy.

The recommended strategy should address:

1. **Long-term in situ conservation.**
2. **Ex situ conservation.**
3. **Medicinal Plant Conservation Areas (MPCAs).**
4. **Long-term availability for local and commercial use.**
5. **Economic incentives for large-scale medicinal-plant cultivation.**
6. **Access to medicinal plants for primary healthcare.**
7. **Scientific research and monitoring.**
8. **Post-harvest handling.**
9. **Value addition.**
10. **Wild-harvest rules and guidelines.**
11. **Cultivation rules and guidelines.**
12. **Domestic and foreign trade rules.**
13. **Market linkages.**
14. **Quality control.**
15. **Awareness generation.**

## 24. Important Caveats for RAG Use

### Source-era legal framework

This document reflects the laws, rules, notifications, and administrative practices as presented in the underlying report. Legal frameworks may have changed after publication. Retrieval systems should therefore surface the **source date/context** whenever answering questions about current compliance.

### State-specific variation

A rule described for one state should not be generalized automatically to another. The chapter repeatedly emphasizes state variation in harvest, cultivation, transit, and trade requirements.

### OCR / botanical-name normalization

The source PDF contains OCR artifacts. Botanical names have been normalized where the intended taxon is reasonably clear, but ambiguous source spellings should remain traceable to the original document.

### Interpretation vs. source fact

Statements such as "need to harmonize transit rules," "urgent need for a national strategy," or recommendations about revising ITC codes are **recommendations/interpretations made by the report**, not neutral descriptions of law.
