# Medicinal Plant Species in Commercial Demand: Consolidated Inventory and Analysis

## Document purpose
This chapter consolidates an inventory of herbal raw drugs and their botanical sources in commercial demand in India, quantifies major demand and trade-value components for 2014–15, and analyzes supply sources, conservation concern, and priority management needs.

## Executive summary
- **1,622 herbal raw-drug entities** were correlated with **1,178 medicinal plant species** in commercial demand for 2014–15.
- Total commercial demand was estimated at **5,12,000 metric tonnes (MT)**, with an estimated trade value of **₹7,000 crore**.
- The demand estimate combines domestic herbal industry consumption, exports, rural-household consumption of commercially traded species, and an estimated 3% handling/storage wastage.
- **242 medicinal plant species** were identified as wild-collected, cultivated, or imported primarily for use as herbal raw drugs at **>100 MT/year**.
- Among the 242 high-demand species, major supply sources were reported as: **54 mainly cultivated, 15 imported, 114 primarily from forests, and 59 from habitats outside forests**; of the 114 forest-sourced species, 36 were from Himalayan forests and 78 from tropical forests.
- The source states that herbal material for **72% of the 242 high-demand species** is sourced entirely or largely from the wild; for species used in classical ASU formulations, the source reports **more than 85% of medicinal plant species** continue to be sourced from the wild.
- The consolidated inventory contains **100 Red-Listed species** in active commercial demand: **36 assessed as Critically Endangered (CR)** and **64 as Endangered (EN)** at regional, national, and/or global levels, according to the cited FRLHT assessment framework.

## Terminology and source conventions
- **Herbal raw drug entity**: a traded/used medicinal-plant material or trade entity that may be associated with one or more botanical sources.
- **Commercial demand**: demand synthesized from domestic herbal industry consumption, exports, rural-household consumption of species in commercial trade, and estimated handling/storage wastage, after the exclusions described in Section 11.2.
- **Trade name ambiguity**: the source highlights one botanical entity having multiple trade names, multiple botanical entities sharing one trade name, and phonetic similarity between local trade names.
- **Equivalent / synonym**: the inventory retains commonly used taxonomic synonyms or trade equivalents to preserve searchability. Example: trade name `Shikakai` commonly associated in trade with *Acacia concinna* is directed to accepted name *Acacia sinuata*.
- **Supply-source codes used in Table 11.7**: `HF` = Himalayan Forests; `TF` = Tropical Forests; `W` = habitats outside forests; `C` = cultivated; `I` = imported.
- **Bracketed quantities** in Table 11.7 represent estimated rural-household consumption in addition to trade quantities.

## Section-by-section knowledge map
### 11.1 Inventory
- Trade names are region-specific and may change along the trade chain, complicating botanical identification and authenticity.
- The inventory builds on Ved and Goraya (2008), which listed 1,289 raw-drug entities correlated to 960 plant species.
- The current study added 218 medicinal species to the previous inventory, attributed mainly to larger sampling of domestic herbal industry units and herbal mandis and improved documentation requirements under Section 157(A) of the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1945 (July 2008).
- 150 species present in the 2008 inventory were not recorded as active commercial trade in the current survey; they were retained as a historical record for future investigation.
- Taxonomic nomenclature was updated using nomenclature followed by the Botanical Survey of India and Plant List (2013), while commonly used synonyms were retained as equivalents.
- Twelve trade-volume ranges were used: `<10`, `10–50`, `50–100`, `100–200`, `200–500`, `500–1,000`, `1,000–2,000`, `2,000–5,000`, `5,000–10,000`, `>10,000`, `>20,000`, `>30,000 MT` (dry weight).
- Top commercial-demand entities with annual trade levels around **20,000 MT or more** included Isabgol (*Plantago ovata*), Senna leaves/pods (*Senna alexandrina*), Chakoda Beej (*Senna tora*), Amla (*Phyllanthus emblica*), Ghritkumari (*Aloe vera*), and Gum Arabic (*Acacia senegal*).

### 11.1.1 Taxonomic and life-form profile
- The 1,178 species/taxa span **781 genera** and **177 families**.
- The source identifies Fabaceae, Asteraceae, and Lamiaceae as the three leading families by number of medicinal plant species in commercial demand; the top ten families together account for about **32%** of the total species.
- Family composition: 159 angiosperm families (136 dicot + 23 monocot), 6 gymnosperm families, 9 pteridophyte families, and 3 families in the fungi/lichen grouping.
- Life-form counts: **314 trees, 200 shrubs, 166 climbers/lianas, 498 herbs including grasses and sedges**; approximate proportions are 27%, 17%, 14%, and 42%, respectively.

### 11.1.2 Plant parts and destructive harvesting
- Roots/rhizomes/tubers/bulbs/root bark account for about **24.8%** of the 1,622 herbal raw-drug entities.
- Wood and stem bark account for **13.9%**; whole plants account for **14.2%**.
- Overall, **52.9%** of the medicinal-plant species in commercial demand require destructive harvesting for the traded raw-drug part. The source notes that the percentage becomes higher when cultivated and imported entities are excluded.

### 11.2 Commercial demand methodology
- Components: domestic herbal industry, exports including gums/extracts, rural-household use of commercially traded species, and 3% wastage during loading/unloading/transport/storage.
- Exclusions: cereals, pulses, fruits, vegetables and vegetable oils for which herbal use is relatively small; and rural/folk-healer species not included in the traded-medicinal-plant inventory.
- All raw-drug entities were converted to dry weight for uniform demand estimates.
- 2014–15 commercial demand was estimated **62% higher in volume** than the comparable 2005–06 estimate by Ved and Goraya (2008); export volume increased from 56,500 MT to 1,34,500 MT, a reported **238% increase**.

### 11.3 Trade value methodology
- The study used an average procurement rate of **₹100/kg** at factory gate for herbal raw drugs consumed by the domestic herbal industry and rural households.
- For exports, the study used the actual export value reported in DGCIS data.
- Total estimated annual trade value: **₹6,985 crore**, rounded to **₹7,000 crore**.
- Export value increased from **₹354.80 crore in 2005–06 to ₹3,211 crore in 2014–15**, described in the source as a nine-fold increase over ten years.

### 11.4 Folk healers / traditional practitioners
- A survey of **89 folk healers/traditional practitioners** documented **583 herbal raw-drug entities corresponding to 386 plant species**.
- **206 species** were also present in the consolidated commercial-demand inventory.
- Practitioners used **1–36 entities**, averaging **6 entities** each.
- Mean annual per-practitioner consumption across all herbal raw-drug entities was **109 kg**, with some practitioners using up to **3,000 kg/year**.
- The top 20 species represented more than **90% of total recorded consumption** among the sampled practitioners; *Withania somnifera* (Ashwagandha) was the highest-use entity.

### 11.5 High commercial demand (>100 MT/year)
- The synthesis identified **1,013 botanical raw-drug entities pertaining to 310 species** with annual trade above 100 MT.
- Of these, 7 species were primarily aromatic/perfumery crops; 57 were primarily spices/cereals/pulses/fruits/vegetables/oil crops; 4 had ambiguous supply sources; and **242 species** were collected, cultivated, or imported primarily for herbal raw-drug use.
- High-demand species are heavily dependent on wild resources, especially for classical ASU raw materials.

### 11.5.3 Ambiguous supply sources
- Four high-consumption entities had unconfirmed supply sources: `Bansalochan/Tabashir` (*Bambusa arundinacea*), `Kapur/Karpura` (*Cinnamomum camphora*), and `Gandhapura Patra Taila` associated with *Gaultheria fragantissima* / *G. procumbens* in the source table.
- The study reports that authentic samples were not obtained during the mandi survey, domestic production was not evident at meaningful scale, corresponding import data were not reflected at the reported magnitude in DGCIS data, and trader discussions suggested a potentially synthetic source. The source therefore marks these supply channels as ambiguous and in need of investigation.

### 11.5.4 Supply-source structure of the 242 high-demand species
- 54 species: major source cultivated.
- 15 species: major source imported.
- 114 species: major source forests, of which 36 Himalayan and 78 tropical.
- 59 species: major source habitats outside forests.
- The source states that 72% of these 242 species are sourced entirely or largely from the wild; for classical ASU formulations, >85% of medicinal-plant species remain wild-sourced.
- Cultivated species such as Aloe vera, Mentha, Isabgol, Henna, and Senna contribute a large share of total volume and are noted as being used heavily in wellness-oriented formulations/raw-drug or extract markets.

### 11.6 Conservation concern
- FRLHT threat-assessment exercises in 18 Indian states, using IUCN Red List Categories and Criteria, reportedly categorised **344 medicinal plant species as threatened** at regional, national, and/or global level.
- The current consolidated commercial inventory includes **100 Red-Listed species**: 36 CR and 64 EN.
- The source notes that nearly half of the CR species are from the Himalayan region; one-fourth of Red-Listed species are trees and another one-fourth are shrubs and large climbers.

### 11.7 Priority management interventions
- **Himalayan forests:** 36 high-trade forest species plus 24 additional Red-Listed Himalayan species in lesser trade were prioritised. The source emphasizes in situ conservation with local community support, sustainable harvest, and targeted cultivation for some herbaceous/high-pressure species.
- **Tropical forests:** 78 high-trade species were identified for priority management, with additional Red-Listed species in lesser trade.
- The source repeatedly distinguishes between trees that should be conserved in natural habitats and species/groups that may be suitable for agroforestry, plantation, or domestication.
- Examples of management issues include declining *Pistacia integerrima* populations/gall formation, declining wild *Zanthoxylum armatum*, destructive lichen harvesting, *Berberis* species substitution under trade names, and pressure on guggul (*Commiphora wightii*) and agarwood (*Aquilaria malaccensis*).

### 11.8 Habitats outside forests
- 59 high-trade medicinal species were identified as self-grown sources in agricultural land, fallow land, roadsides, canal banks, ponds/lakes, and wastelands.
- The source warns that intensification of agriculture is shrinking some habitats; over-collection is also reducing availability for species such as *Cissus quadrangularis* and *Cardiospermum halicacabum*.
- Pollution from fertilisers, pesticides, fungicides, weedicides, industrial effluents, and sewage can make medicinal plants from these habitats unsuitable as herbal raw drugs.
- Pilot cultivation was reported for species including *Bacopa monnieri*, *Centella asiatica*, *Cyperus scariosus*, *Phyllanthus amarus*, and *Solanum nigrum* to obtain authentic, uncontaminated material.

### 11.9 Limitations
- Inventory quality depends on sampling and field verification and is constrained by use of equivalents and substitutions in trade.
- Example: the raw-drug name `Bala` may refer to multiple *Sida* species; species-specific trade volumes therefore could not always be separated.
- Some small-volume trade entities could not be correlated to exact botanical identities and were excluded.
- The study compares only 2004–05 and 2014–15 datasets; annual fluctuations between these points cannot be inferred from the two studies alone.

## Source-attributed conservation and management observations
- The source argues that continued high use of wild-collected raw drugs, together with habitat degradation, is placing pressure on wild medicinal-plant populations.
- It recommends species-specific combinations of in situ conservation, sustainable harvesting, plantation/agroforestry, cultivation, genetic-resource conservation, and improved resource management rather than a single conservation approach for all species.
- The chapter highlights the importance of preserving authenticity and traceability because several commercially important trade names represent multiple botanical sources or rely on accepted-vs-trade synonym handling.

## RAG caveats
- **Time scope:** All quantitative demand and trade values in this chapter refer to **2014–15** unless explicitly stated otherwise.
- **Not current market data:** The chapter is a historical assessment; figures should not be treated as current 2026 market volumes/prices without external verification.
- **Threat status:** The source’s Red-List labels are tied to the assessment scope and methodology described in the chapter; Table 11.10b explicitly notes that its threat status is limited to the specific states assessed and does not represent global Red List status except specified endemic cases.
- **Taxonomy:** The inventory uses updated botanical nomenclature while retaining trade synonyms/equivalents. Do not assume that every trade-name synonym is a taxonomically accepted synonym.
- **Extraction quality:** The PDF text layer contains recurring glyph-loss artifacts. Obvious English and botanical-name artifacts have been normalized where the intended term is clear; uncertain source-era names have not been silently modernized.
