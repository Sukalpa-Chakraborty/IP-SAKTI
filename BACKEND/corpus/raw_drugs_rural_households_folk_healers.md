# Raw Drugs Consumption by Rural Household and Folk Healers

## RAG Document Profile
- **Source chapter:** Chapter 4 of *Medicinal Plants: An Assessment of their Demand and Supply in India*.
- **Core population:** Rural households and folk healers/traditional practitioners in India.
- **Reference period for the main consumption estimates:** 2014-15.
- **Rural household survey:** 2,450 households across 15 states, using multistage sampling.
- **Medicinal plant diversity recorded among surveyed rural households:** 479 species, represented by 677 herbal raw-drug entities.
- **Estimated national annual herbal raw-drug consumption by rural households:** 171,500 MT in 2014-15.
- **Folk healer survey:** 89 respondents; 340 medicinal plant species documented; estimated annual consumption 9.82 MT across the respondents.
- **Primary source publication authors:** G. S. Goraya, D. K. Ved, Vaneet Jishtu, R. S. Rawat, and Shilpa Gautam.

## Key Retrieval Facts
### Rural households
The chapter states that nearly 138 million rural households existed in India for the study base year. The survey recorded 479 medicinal plant species in use among sampled rural households. National consumption of herbal raw drugs by this user segment was estimated at 1,71,500 MT in 2014-15. Of the recorded species, 296 species were described as being in active commercial trade and accounted for about 94% of total annual consumption by weight.

### Folk healers
The study interviewed 89 folk healers identified using prior information during the household survey. Their use covered 340 medicinal plant species. Combined annual consumption for the 89 responding healers was estimated at 9.82 MT, equivalent to about 109 kg per healer per year on average. The document also states that 202 of the 340 species were in active commercial trade.

### Data interpretation cautions
The study emphasizes botanical-identity challenges because local and trade names vary by region. Species that could not be sufficiently correlated with accepted botanical identities were excluded from the documented dataset. The national rural-household estimates were obtained by calculating average consumption from sampled households within zones and extrapolating those values to the corresponding household populations.

## 4.1 Introduction

India is described as having highly varied traditional systems of medicine, including oral and codified practices integrated with local culture. Rural communities use nearby botanicals for primary health care, while villages commonly have folk healers or traditional practitioners who specialize in one or more ailments. Healing herbs are collected locally or purchased from retail shops. The chapter cites an FRLHT database estimate of about 6,500 plant species used by such communities across Indian states. The present study revisits earlier estimates of consumption of herbal raw drugs by rural households and folk healers, following an earlier estimate by Ved and Goraya (2008) of approximately 86,000 MT of annual consumption for the rural household segment.

## 4.2 Estimation of Annual Consumption of Medicinal Plants by Rural Households

### Survey design and sampling
National Census 2011 data were used as the base for planning the rural household sample. Approximately 138 million households, or 71.16% of the total households in the country, were recorded as rural. The survey used multistage sampling with zone, state, district, village, and household as successive stages/units. Consumption information was collected with pre-tested semi-structured questionnaires. A total of 2,450 rural households in 15 states were surveyed.

### Botanical identity and data-collection method
The study reports that establishing the identity of medicinal plants named by rural communities was a major challenge. Local names were matched against ethno-medicinal literature, local floras, expert consultation, and, where possible, physical plant specimens and herbarium specimens. Quantitative and qualitative data included local raw-drug name, plant part used, annual quantity, collection source, and purchase price. Zone-level household averages were extrapolated to all households in the zone and then aggregated to national estimates.

### Findings from the rural-household dataset

- Six-zone analysis reported the greatest diversity of medicinal plant use in the North Eastern Zone, followed by the Southern, Central, North Western, Northern, and Western Zones.
- Eight species were reported as used across all six zones: Ocimum tenuiflorum (tulasi), Phyllanthus emblica (amla), Aegle marmelos (bael), Azadirachta indica (neem), Moringa oleifera (sahjan), Terminalia bellirica (bahera), Asparagus racemosus (shatavari), and Aloe vera (ghritkumari).
- Ten additional species were reported across five zones, including Calotropis procera, Catharanthus roseus, Terminalia chebula, Ficus religiosa, Justicia adhatoda, Mentha arvensis, Phyllanthus amarus, Syzygium cumini, and Vitex negundo; the source text describes the remaining listed species as occurring in three or fewer zones.
- Average annual consumption of dry herbal raw drugs per rural household was estimated at 1.24 kg.
- Of the 296 medicinal plant species in active trade, 109 had annual rural-household consumption above 100 MT; these 109 species were stated to account for 92% of total annual consumption at the rural-household level.
- The source identifies 11 species with consumption above 3,000 MT/year at the rural-household level: Ocimum tenuiflorum, Phyllanthus emblica, Aegle marmelos, Azadirachta indica, Moringa oleifera, Terminalia bellirica, Terminalia chebula, Cissus quadrangularis, Cardiospermum halicacabum, Nyctanthes arbor-tristis, and Aloe vera.
- The 479 documented species belonged to 128 plant families. Dominant families named in the text include Asteraceae, Lamiaceae, Fabaceae, Malvaceae, Euphorbiaceae, Rubiaceae, Acanthaceae, Apocynaceae, Solanaceae, Caesalpiniaceae, Convolvulaceae, Mimosaceae, and Phyllanthaceae.
- Part-wise analysis of 677 herbal raw-drug entities reported leaf as 28% and root/rhizome/tuber as 18%; whole plant accounted for 13%; fruits and stem 9%; bark 7%; seeds 6%; flowers 4%; and less than 1% each for several remaining categories. The source notes that whole plants, roots, stems, and bark together represented about 47% of use and therefore involved potentially destructive harvesting.
- Frequently mentioned health problems included digestive/abdominal disorders, post-delivery care, snake bite, body injuries, common cold, flu, cough, and wounds. The source records that the same plant may be used for multiple ailments and different plant parts may be used for different populations or purposes.

### Chewing sticks for oral healthcare
The chapter describes chewing sticks as widely used herbal raw drugs for dental and oral healthcare in both rural and urban areas. Plant parts used include stem, bark, root, or leaf. The source lists neem (Azadirachta indica), babool (Acacia nilotica), meswak (Salvadora oleoides), dandasa (Juglans regia), paja (Prunus cerasoides), bashal (Salix tetrasperma), basut (Adhatoda zeylanica), gandhla (Murraya koenigii), puthkanda (Achyranthes aspera), and timber (Zanthoxylum armatum) among species documented for this purpose.

## 4.3 Source of Medicinal Plants / Raw Drugs

The rural communities were reported to collect most medicinal plant species from nearby forests, field bunds, fallow lands, and wastelands. Some species were cultivated in homesteads and some purchased from markets. The survey data are summarized in the source as more than 80% of medicinal plants (excluding fruits, vegetables, cereals, and spices) being collected from local forests and non-forest habitats, about 10% sourced from homestead cultivation, and less than 10% purchased from markets because they were not available locally.

## 4.4 Estimation of Herbal Raw Drug Consumption by Folk Healers

Based on prior information obtained during the household survey, 89 folk healers were interviewed. The study documented 340 medicinal plant species, excluding vegetables, fruits, spices, cereals, and pulses, used in day-to-day dispensing. Total annual consumption among the 89 respondents was estimated at 9.82 MT, with an average of approximately 109 kg per folk healer per year.

### Folk-healer consumption patterns and interpretation
The chapter reports that 340 medicinal plants used by folk healers belonged to 114 plant families. Asteraceae, Fabaceae, Lamiaceae, Euphorbiaceae, Malvaceae, Apocynaceae, and Solanaceae were identified as dominant families, each represented by more than 10 species. In total, 517 herbal raw-drug entities corresponding to the 340 species were documented. Leaves (24%) and roots (17%) were the major raw-drug parts, followed by whole plant (11%), stem and bark (9%), fruit (8%), and seeds (6%); the remaining entities included flower, latex/gum/exudates, wood, gall, and root bark. The source states that 46% of the raw-drug entities involved whole plants, roots, stem, and bark, indicating potentially destructive wild harvesting.

The survey also found that some healers used different parts of the same plant under different names for different ailments. Examples described include different parts of neem (Azadirachta indica), multiple medicinal preparations from amaltas (Cassia fistula), and multiple uses of sohanjan (Moringa oleifera). Other plants mentioned in comparable multi-use contexts include Aegle marmelos, Hibiscus rosa-sinensis, Cinnamomum obtusifolia, and Acacia catechu. The source notes a preference among many folk healers for fresh material and nearby local plants.

## Medicinal Plants in the Practice of Sowa Rigpa

The source reports an estimated 500 plant species used in the practice of Sowa Rigpa in the trans-Himalayas. An interaction with Dr. Tsering Phunstog, Chief Amchi, LAHDC Leh, is described as indicating declining availability of many medicinal plants traditionally collected from cold-desert areas. A field exercise around South Pullu en route to Khardung La reportedly yielded about 70 medicinal plant species in a 2-hour collection exercise in 2000, compared with about 25-30 species in a repeat exercise in 2016. The cited practitioner attributed the decline primarily to changing climatic conditions, specifically reduced winter snow and negligible summer rains, rather than over-harvesting by Amchis.

The chapter records the Amchi principles 'Ska sar Skei-wa' (right location) and 'Tus Su Turva' (right time) for wild collection and 'Soma Ning Pa' as a prescribed shelf-life practice intended to reduce overharvesting by requiring disposal of herbs after their prescribed shelf life.

The source also describes conservation activities supported by the Ladakh Society for Traditional Medicine (LSTM), including awareness work, community organization, a Medicinal Plant Conservation Area near Mahe in Changthang, and community reserves at Rangdom Village in Zanskar and Sapi Village in Kargil. Species explicitly mentioned in the conservation context include Dactylorhiza hatagirea, Aconitum heterophyllum, Dracocephalum spp., and Arnebia euchroma.

Dr. Tenzin Thaye is quoted in the source as reporting challenges obtaining authentic material for roughly 200 other medicinal plant species used regularly by Amchis and stating that about 90% of an estimated annual requirement of 15-20 MT of herbal raw drugs was met through market purchases from Majith Mandi, Amritsar, and Khari Baoli, Delhi. The source further reports about 250 Amchis in Ladakh and another 70 in Himachal Pradesh; individual annual herb consumption was described as varying around 40-50 kg for routine practice, while some larger-scale medicine preparation was reported at 4-5 MT per year and the Ladakh Amchi Sabha preparation centre at about 10 MT per year.

## Field Survey Highlights from Himachal Pradesh

The chapter records beliefs, myths, and collection rituals associated with medicinal plants. Examples include fixed collection days or periods for particular plants, consultation with local deities, and community rules on who may collect particular species. The document gives examples involving Angelica glauca (chora), Picrorhiza kurroa (karu), Saussurea obvallata (Brahmkamal), and Datisca cannabina (bajar bhang). It also records a general NW Himalayan belief that medicinal plants are most appropriately collected after the end of August and that collection timing and location affect perceived medicinal strength.

4.1. INTRODUCTION
India has one of the oldest as well as one of the most varied traditional systems of medicine, both
oral and codified, which are completely integrated with its culture. The various ethnic
communities in the country have been using the botanicals from the nearby forests since times
immemorial for their primary health care needs. To help address more serious health care issues,
almost every village in the country also has folk healers/ traditional practitioners, oOen
specialising in treating one or more ailments. The major mainstay of these practices is healing
herbs collected either from the nearby areas or purchased from the retail shops. These
communities and folk healers hold a phenomenal knowledge about the use of locally available
plants for health care. Till date use of about 6,500 plant species by these communities across
various States in the country has been documented (FRLHT database). The quantity of material
used by this very important segment of the users of medicinal plants is believed to be very
significant. Many of the plant species used by this segment of users is also in active trade. This
brings in an issue of resource management. Till recently, consumption of medicinal plants by this
important user group was not factored-in while estimating demand and supply of medicinal plants
in the country. It was only aOer Ved and Goraya (2008), working on a very limited rural household
sample, flagged the issue and estimated the total annual consumption of herbal raw drugs by this
user segment at 86,000 MT that competitive consumption of medicinal plant species in actice
trade has started gaining importance.
The present study was taken up with a view to revisit the previous estimates of consumption of
herbal raw drugs by rural households and the folk healers.

scarce. It was also noted that some plant species, in less frequent use, remained very important as

knowledgeable regarding common home remedies, and it was them who were managing the

Chewing Sticks for Oral Healthcare
Chewing sticks are one of the most widely used herbal
raw drugs for dental and oral healthcare across the
country, both in rural and urban areas. Taken from
various plant parts viz. stem, bark, root or leaf of various
plant species, chewing sticks are used as tooth brushes
and are chewed several times daily to clean and freshen
teeth, strengthen gums and also to mitigate digestive
problems. The use of chewing sticks is thus both
preventative and curative. A number of plant species
used for dental care and care of oral cavity were
documented during the survey of households. Some of
the commonly used species for this purpose include
'neem' (Azadirachta indica), 'babool' (Acacia nilotica),

regia), 'paja' (Prunus cerasoides), 'bashal' (Salix

characteristic taste and flavour (bitter, sweet, sour, astringent and antiseptic) and qualities
(hard and soO) and the local people use different chewing sticks for taking care of teeth, oral
hygiene and digestion problems.

4.3. SOURCE OF MEDICINAL PLANTS / RAW DRUGS
The rural communities have been traditionally collecting most of the medicinal plant species from
nearby forests and from field bunds and fallow lands/ wastelands. Some of the species are also
cultivated in the homesteads and some are purchased from market too. Survey data from rural
households reveals that the communities were collecting more than 80% of the medicinal plants
(excluding fruits, vegetables, cereals and spices) from the local forests and non-forest habitats,
and about 10% of the medicinal plants were being sourced from cultivation in the homesteads.
Only the remaining less than 10% of the medicinal plants, not available locally, are purchased from
the market.

and data about their usage of herbal raw drugs for treating various ailments recorded.
Collation of data has resulted in enlisting of 340 medicinal plant species (excluding vegetables,
fruits, spices, cereals and pulses) used by the surveyed 89 folk healers for their day-to-day
dispensation (table 4.4). Total annual consumption of medicinal plants by 89 responding folk
healers has been estimated to be 9.82 MT with an average estimated per folk healer annual
consumption of about 109 kg.

Medicinal Plants in the Practice of Sowa Rigpa
An estimated 500 plant species are reported to be used in the practice of Sowa rigpa, an
Indian System of Medicine prevalent in the trans-Himalayas. Many of these species are
collected from the region. An interaction with Dr. Tsering Phunstog, Chief Amchi, LAHDC,
Leh (09419887650/ 08493846494) revealed that the availability of most of the medicinal
plants traditionally collected from the cold desert areas has declined over the years. Citing
the example of one workshop conducted for Amchis in 2000, he informed that during a 2

were able to collect about 70 medicinal plants species. The repeat of that exercise in 2016
has resulted in collection of merely 25-30 medicinal plant
species from the same area over the same time. He
opined that the major reason for this decline was fast
changing climatic conditions - the area was receiving
much less winter snow and negligible summer rains - and
not over-harvesting by the Amchis. He said that Amchis

the wild collections. He further informed that the Swa

completed their prescribed shelf life.

Some Interesting Highlights about Medicinal Plants from the Field Survey
The field survey in Himachal Pradesh, in addition to recording usage of herbal raw drugs,
resulted in documentation of interesting information about the belief and myths around
medicinal plants. At many places gathering and use of medicinal plants is linked to some
rituals. For example, some plants are to be collected only at certain times of the day, in a
certain month or from a specific location.
- In Surd village of Rampur Forest Division, the 'chora' (Angelica glauca) roots are
collected only on one day during May in the entire year. Permission is sought from the
local deity and a day is fixed when the villagers move together into the forest to collect
their yearly supply of 'chora'.
- In the remote Janglik village of Rohru, the collection of 'karu' (Picrorhiza kurroa) is
regulated by the local deity and a time is fixed when the village folk move to camp in the
higher altitudes for collection/ harvest of 'karu'. The area for collection and time is also
specified for conservation of the species.
- In the Baspa Valley of Kinnaur, the 'Brahmkamal' (Saussurea obvallata) is collected aOer
the third week of July and that too by the unmarried youth of the village. It is a
ceremonial affair and first the village deity is consulted, a day is fixed and then the youth
move into the higher alpine slopes for collection. On return the flowers are first offered
to the local deity.
- In Lahaul, it is believed that no male member of a household can uproot the plant of
'bajar bhang' (Datisca cannabina) unless he is the only male member in the household.
Moreover, in the NW Himalayas, as a general rule, most of the medicinal plants are
collected aOer 'bees bhadon' (end August). The folk healers and locals believe that the
medicinal properties are less active if they are gathered earlier. The timing and manner in
which plant medicines are collected determine their medicinal power. Also, there is a
general belief that the medicinal plants that are collected from deep inside the forest are
more powerful and effective.
