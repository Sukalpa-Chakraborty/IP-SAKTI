# Patenting Bar under Sections 3(j) and 3(p) of the Patents Act, 1970

## 1. Document overview

This note collects, for retrieval, the parts of Indian patent law that most often decide whether an Ayurvedic innovation is patentable: the exclusions in **section 3 of the Patents Act, 1970** (especially **3(j)** — plants and animals and their parts, and **3(p)** — traditional knowledge), the Indian Patent Office's **Guidelines for Examination of AYUSH Related Inventions (2025)**, and the role of the **Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL)** in examination.

| Item | Position |
|---|---|
| Statute | The Patents Act, 1970 (as amended), section 3 — "What are not inventions" |
| Rules | Patents Rules, 2003 (as amended, including the Patent Amendment Rules, 2024) |
| Guidance | Guidelines for Examination of AYUSH Related Inventions, Indian Patent Office, 2025 (reports of October 2025); earlier framework: Guidelines for Processing of Patent Applications Relating to Traditional Knowledge and Biological Material (2012) |
| Prior-art infrastructure | Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL), CSIR–National Chemical Laboratory with the Department of Ayush |
| Related treaty | WIPO Treaty on Intellectual Property, Genetic Resources and Traditional Knowledge (GRATK), 2024 |

**Why it matters:** the two most common reasons an Ayurveda-derived filing fails in India are (a) the claim is directed to the plant, a plant part, or a known use of a known herbal material (**3(j)** / novelty and inventive step), and (b) the claim reads on what the classical texts already teach (**3(p)**, read with the TKDL prior-art corpus that examiners actually search).

## 2. Section 3 — the exclusions, as they read

Section 3 begins: *"The following are not inventions within the meaning of this Act..."* The clauses most often engaged by Ayurveda filings:

| Clause | Text (condensed from the Act) |
|---|---|
| **3(a)** | An invention which is frivolous or which claims anything obviously contrary to well established natural laws |
| **3(b)** | An invention whose primary or intended use or commercial exploitation could be contrary to public order or morality, or which causes serious prejudice to human, animal or plant life or health or to the environment |
| **3(c)** | The mere discovery of a scientific principle or the formulation of an abstract theory, or discovery of any living thing or non-living substance occurring in nature |
| **3(d)** | The mere discovery of a **new form of a known substance which does not result in the enhancement of the known efficacy** of that substance, or the mere discovery of any new property or new use for a known substance, or of the mere use of a known process, machine or apparatus unless such known process results in a new product or employs at least one new reactant. **Explanation:** salts, esters, ethers, polymorphs, metabolites, pure form, particle size, isomers, mixtures of isomers, complexes, combinations and other derivatives of a known substance are considered **the same substance**, unless they differ significantly in properties with regard to efficacy |
| **3(e)** | A substance obtained by a **mere admixture** resulting only in the aggregation of the properties of the components, or a process for producing such substance |
| **3(f)** | The mere arrangement or re-arrangement or duplication of known devices each functioning independently of one another in a known way |
| **3(h)** | A method of agriculture or horticulture |
| **3(i)** | Any process for the medicinal, surgical, curative, prophylactic, diagnostic, therapeutic or other treatment of human beings, or any process for a similar treatment of animals to render them free of disease or to increase their economic value or that of their products |
| **3(j)** | **Plants and animals in whole or any part thereof other than micro organisms but including seeds, varieties and species and essentially biological processes for production or propagation of plants and animals** |
| **3(k)** | A mathematical or business method or a computer programme per se or algorithms |
| **3(m)** | A mere scheme or rule or method of performing mental act or method of playing game |
| **3(n)** | A presentation of information |
| **3(o)** | Topography of integrated circuits |
| **3(p)** | **An invention which in effect, is traditional knowledge or which is an aggregation or duplication of known properties of traditionally known component or components** |

(Clause 3(g) stands omitted by the Patents (Amendment) Act, 2002. The wording above is condensed for retrieval; quote from the consolidated Act.)

## 3. Section 3(j) — the plant and animal bar

**What it excludes:** plants and animals *in whole or any part thereof*, other than micro-organisms, expressly including seeds, varieties and species, and essentially biological processes for their production or propagation.

**How it is applied to Ayurvedic subject matter:**

1. **A claim to a plant part as such** — root, bark, leaf, gum, extract presented as the material itself — runs into 3(j). A claim drafted as "a *Withania somnifera* root" or "a dried plant part" is not an invention even before novelty is reached.
2. **Micro-organisms are carved out.** A novel, non-obvious microbial strain or a fermentation organism is not excluded by 3(j) (subject to the rest of section 3 and the usual tests).
3. **Isolated/extracted material is a boundary, not a safe harbour.** The IPO's AYUSH guidance states the position directly: where a claim is to an **extract, alkaloid or active ingredient derived from a plant for a specific purpose and the plant is already used for that purpose, the claim lacks novelty and inventive step — but the *process* used to obtain it can be patented if it meets the novelty and inventive-step requirements.** Whether a product claim to an isolated substance clears 3(j) is claim- and decision-specific; do not assume it does.
4. **Essentially biological processes are excluded**, while a technical process applied to biological material (extraction, standardisation, stabilisation, formulation, delivery) is the direction in which filings usually succeed.
5. **Plant variety protection is elsewhere.** Varieties and species are the subject matter of the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act, 2001 and (for breeder's rights) international UPOV-style systems; the patent route is deliberately closed to them in India.

**Drafting responses that survive 3(j):** claim the *process* (extraction, fractionation, standardisation, purification, drying, formulation, delivery system) rather than the material; claim a defined composition with technical features (ratios, particle size, excipient system, controlled-release matrix) rather than the botanical list; claim the device or apparatus; claim a formulation with a demonstrated unexpected effect supported by data.

## 4. Section 3(p) — the traditional knowledge bar

**Text:** an invention which **in effect is traditional knowledge**, or which is an **aggregation or duplication of known properties of traditionally known components**.

Two limbs, both routinely invoked against Ayurvedic filings:

1. **"In effect is traditional knowledge."** If the invention, stripped of its drafting, is what a classical text or a practising vaidya already knows, the exclusion applies regardless of how the claim is written. The relevant comparison is against the knowledge as it exists — which is why the TKDL matters at the examination stage.
2. **Aggregation or duplication of known properties of traditionally known components.** A multi-ingredient Ayurvedic composition where each ingredient's known property is merely listed side by side is aggregation, not invention. This is the same concern that 3(e) (mere admixture) expresses chemically and that the AYUSH Guidelines express through the **synergism** requirement: the combination must do **more than the sum of its parts**, and that has to be shown with data.

**Note on numbering:** 3(p) is the current home of the traditional-knowledge exclusion. Older commentary and some non-consolidated texts cite "section 3(a)" for traditional knowledge; check which text you are reading before citing a clause.

**Where 3(p) meets 3(d).** Isolating a known constituent, making a new physical form, or finding a new use for a classical formulation will usually be met by 3(d) (no enhanced efficacy) as well as 3(p). The Explanation to 3(d) means salts, esters, polymorphs, metabolites, particle-size variants, isomers, complexes and combinations of a known substance are *the same substance* unless efficacy differs significantly.

## 5. The 2025 AYUSH examination guidelines

The Indian Patent Office's **Guidelines for Examination of AYUSH Related Inventions (2025)** are the operative examination framework for Ayurveda, Yoga & Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, Sowa-Rigpa and Homeopathy filings. As reported in practitioner summaries of the published PDF:

**Legal provisions stressed**

- Compliance with both the **Patents Act, 1970 / Patents Rules, 2003** and the **Biological Diversity Act, 2002**.
- Patentability under section 2 requirements, and avoidance of the section 3 exclusions — with **3(e) (mere admixture)** and **3(p) (traditional knowledge)** called out as particularly important.
- **Prior approval of the National Biodiversity Authority** where Indian biological resources are used (the approval obligation is placed under the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 — reported as section 6 of that Act); failure can result in **refusal, opposition or revocation**.
- Disclosure of the **source and biological material** used; wrong or non-disclosure is treated as a ground for refusal, opposition or revocation.

**Process**

1. Filing — full disclosure of the invention, including the biological material used.
2. Screening and classification — AYUSH-related applications are marked and routed to examiners with the relevant background; publication after 18 months.
3. Examination — novelty and inventive step searched, including against the **TKDL** and other AYUSH/TK databases.

**The six guidelines (as summarised)**

| # | Point | Carve-out |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A claim to an extract, alkaloid or active ingredient from a plant for a purpose the plant is already used for is **not novel or inventive** | The *processes* for obtaining it can be patented if they meet the tests |
| 2 | Combinations of plants or of mineral/animal-origin ingredients, or of existing formulations with known therapeutic uses, are **obvious** | Inventive where **synergism** is proved — combined effect exceeds the sum of the individual effects |
| 3 | A combination containing an ingredient **known to treat a particular disease** is presumed obvious for that same disease | May be non-obvious if an **unexpected technical or synergistic effect** is shown |
| 4 | Finding a **workable range or optimum quantity** of known traditional ingredients by routine experimentation is **not patentable** | A range can be inventive where it produces unexpected or non-obvious technical effects |
| 5 | Selecting **one or more ingredients from a known multi-ingredient formula** to treat the same disease is **not inventive** | An **unexpected or improved result** for the selected ingredient can be inventive |
| 6 | **Equipment and devices** used for known AYUSH procedures may be patentable | If they satisfy the general criteria (novelty, inventive step, industrial applicability) |

Annexures reportedly illustrate **synergism** with worked examples; the Guidelines also name reference databases: the **Ayush Research Portal**, the **TKDL**, the **Indian Medicinal Plant Database**, **e-Charkha** and the **Tribal Digital Repository**.

## 6. The Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL)

| Item | Position |
|---|---|
| What it is | A structured, classified digital corpus of knowledge from Ayurveda, Siddha, Unani, Sowa-Rigpa and Yoga, transcribed from classical texts and traditional formulations into a format patent examiners can search |
| Who runs it | CSIR–National Chemical Laboratory with the Department of Ayush |
| Content | Reported at **34 million pages** (WIPO, 2011); formulations/practices numbering in the hundreds of thousands; content translated/classified for search (English plus other languages, indexed under the Traditional Knowledge Resource Classification) |
| Access model | **Restricted.** Access is granted to patent offices under **Access and Non-Disclosure Agreements**, not to the public |
| Milestones | Access agreements with the **European Patent Office (February 2009)** and **USPTO (July 2009)**; further offices subsequently (Japan, Korea, WIPO and others reported); **Danish Patent and Trademark Office** agreement reported August 2022; **Cabinet approval (August 2022) to widen access beyond patent offices**; an **India–Brazil** access agreement reported |
| Public face | `https://www.tkdl.res.in` (official CSIR-TKDL site) and `https://www.csir.res.in` |

**What this means in practice:**

- Examiners in India and in several foreign offices **do** search the TKDL. A claim that is merely the classical teaching will be found.
- The TKDL is **not publicly searchable**. Any product, agent or corpus that claims to search it is either working under an agreement or is mis-describing its access. In this platform the TKDL is treated as a **restricted** source: identified, never accessed, never reproduced.
- For an applicant, the practical substitute is the **public** classical literature — the texts themselves (Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita, Ashtanga Hridayam, Bhavaprakasha Nighantu and others listed in the First Schedule to the Drugs and Cosmetics Act), the Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India, published research, and patent databases.

## 7. A prior-art search strategy that matches how examiners search

1. **Search the claim, not the product name.** Convert the claim to its technical features: species (botanical name), plant part, extraction solvent, ratio, process conditions, delivery form, and the asserted effect.
2. **Search the botanical synonyms.** Vernacular names, Sanskrit names, synonyms in classical texts, and botanical synonyms/genus synonyms all matter; a single species can carry dozens of names.
3. **Search Indian patent literature** (IP India public search) and the **Patent Journal**, plus global databases (EPO/WIPO/USPTO family and non-patent literature).
4. **Search the classical literature directly** — the same texts the TKDL is built from, in translation where needed.
5. **Search the pharmacopoeia and monograph layer** — Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India, WHO monographs, ICMR quality standards: a monograph establishing a use destroys novelty of that use.
6. **Search traditional-knowledge and biodiversity open resources** — the Ayush Research Portal, Indian Medicinal Plant Database, and open TK repositories.
7. **Search foreign-language literature** where the target market is non-Indian (Chinese medicine, Unani-Arabic, European herbal) — novelty is global.
8. **Document the search** — date, databases, queries, and results. The record supports the inventive-step argument and any later opposition defence.
9. **Search the applications as well as granted rights** — pending applications are prior art for novelty in most systems and are relevant to inventive step.

## 8. Historical illustrations (well-documented)

| Matter | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Turmeric** — US patent on the use of turmeric powder for wounds (US 5,401,504) | Challenged by India (CSIR) in the 1990s; the patent was revoked/abandoned after the challenge — the classic "patent on known use" failure |
| **Neem** — European patent on a neem-based fungicidal process (EP 0436094) | Opposed by India and others; the EPO Opposition Division revoked the patent after long proceedings — the classic "known use of a known material" failure |
| **Basmati** — RiceTec's claims on basmati rice lines | USPTO rejected or narrowed a substantial part of the claims — the classic "varieties and species" failure |
| **Panchagavya / herbal lamp** — reported Madras High Court decision | The court is reported to have upheld rejection of a patent application for an eco-friendly lamp made from panchagavya and herbal leaves, on the ground that the claims were in substance biological/known-knowledge — verify the citation and outcome before relying on it |

These are *illustrations of the doctrine*, not a substitute for a search.

## 9. Interfaces with other regimes

| Regime | Interface |
|---|---|
| **Biological Diversity Act, 2002** (as amended 2023) and the **Biological Diversity Rules, 2024** | Prior NBA approval/registration before filing, and the 45-day post-grant intimation, are separate duties from the patent filing itself (see `biological-diversity-rules-2024-india.md`) |
| **Disclosure of origin** | The Patents Act and rules require the source and geographical origin of biological material to be disclosed in the application; incorrect or missing disclosure is a ground of refusal/opposition/revocation — described here by subject, verify the exact provisions |
| **WIPO GRATK Treaty, 2024** | Adds an international **disclosure-of-origin** obligation for inventions based on genetic resources or associated traditional knowledge: applicants disclose the country of origin or source and, where applicable, the indigenous peoples/local community; parties give an opportunity to correct and may provide guidance; **no obligation for applications filed before the treaty enters into force for that Party**; entry into force **3 months after 15 eligible parties** deposit ratification/accession; review after 4 years |
| **PPV&FR Act, 2001** | Varieties are registrable there, not patentable under 3(j) |
| **Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940** | Patenting does not substitute for a manufacturing licence or pharmacopoeial compliance |
| **Secrecy/trade secret** | Filing publishes at 18 months; for know-how that cannot support a claim, contract and confidentiality are the alternative |

## 10. Common failure modes

1. Claiming the plant, a plant part, or an extract with no technical process features — **3(j)**.
2. Claiming a known use of a classical formulation in a new physical form — **3(d)** (no enhanced efficacy) and/or **3(p)**.
3. Listing ingredients with their known properties — **3(p)** aggregation / **3(e)** admixture; no synergism data.
4. Claiming a treatment method — **3(i)**.
5. Choosing a "workable range" by routine optimisation and presenting it as inventive.
6. Selecting one ingredient out of a known multi-herb formula for the same therapeutic purpose.
7. Missing or wrong biological-source disclosure, or filing before the NBA approval/registration — refusal, opposition, revocation risk independent of the merits.
8. Assuming foreign filings escape the same analysis — 3(d)/3(p) reasoning is applied by Indian examiners, and foreign offices search TKDL under access agreements.
9. Treating TKDL as a searchable prior-art source in a product: it is restricted; the public substitutes are the classical texts, pharmacopoeias and published literature.

## 11. What an Ayurvedic product owner must check

- [ ] Is the claim to a **process, device, composition with technical features, or delivery system** rather than to the plant or a plant part?
- [ ] If a product claim is intended, has it been tested against **3(j)** on the current case law and the AYUSH Guidelines?
- [ ] Has the claim been read against the **classical source text** for the recipe/use (and is the reference recorded)?
- [ ] Is there **data** showing synergism or an unexpected technical effect for the combination, range or selection?
- [ ] Does the invention add anything beyond a **new form or new use** of a known substance (3(d))?
- [ ] Is any **treatment method** claim removed or reworded (3(i))?
- [ ] Is the **biological source and geographical origin** disclosed accurately in the specification?
- [ ] Is the **NBA approval/registration** in place *before* filing (see the biodiversity file), and the 45-day post-grant intimation diarised?
- [ ] Has a **documented prior-art search** been run across patents, pharmacopoeias, classical texts and TK-adjacent open databases?
- [ ] Is the **18-month publication** acceptable for the know-how in the application — what should be held back as trade secret?
- [ ] For foreign filing, has **foreign filing licence / PCT** routing and any **GRATK/disclosure-of-origin** requirement in the target country been checked?
- [ ] Does the claim set avoid banned conclusions — no assertion that a claim *is* patentable before an examiner says so?
