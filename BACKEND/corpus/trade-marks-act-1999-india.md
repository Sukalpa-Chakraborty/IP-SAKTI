# The Trade Marks Act, 1999 and the Trade Marks Rules, 2017

## 1. Document overview

The Trade Marks Act, 1999 consolidates and amends the law relating to trade marks, provides for registration and protection of trade marks, and for prevention of the use of false and misleading marks. It is read with the Trade Marks Rules, 2017 (as amended), which prescribe forms, fees and procedure.

| Item | Position |
|---|---|
| Enactment | The Trade Marks Act, 1999 (in force from 15 September 2003; verify commencement particulars) |
| Subordinate legislation | Trade Marks Rules, 2017 (as amended) |
| Register | Register of Trade Marks maintained by the Trade Marks Registry under the Registrar of Trade Marks (Controller General of Patents, Designs and Trade Marks) |
| Offices | Principal Office plus branch offices (Mumbai, Kolkata, Delhi, Ahmedabad, Chennai) — verify current list |
| Register structure | Single register under the 1999 Act (the Part A / Part B structure of the earlier 1958 Act is gone) |

**Why it matters for Ayurveda:** a brand name/logo is usually the most valuable and most attainable IP asset for an Ayurvedic product company. Formulations are rarely patentable (section 3(p)) and GIs rarely fit — but a distinctive brand, protected under this Act, is what customers actually buy.

## 2. Definition of trade mark — Section 2(1)(zb)

**"Trade mark"** means a mark capable of being represented graphically and capable of distinguishing the goods or services of one person from those of others, and **includes a shape of goods, their packaging and combination of colours.**

Decoded:

- **Graphically representable** — words, logos, devices, labels, packaging shapes, combinations of colours (as applied to goods), sound marks (representable in staff notation/graphical form), and increasingly other non-traditional marks accepted by practice.
- **Distinguishing** — it must function as a source identifier, not merely describe.
- **Shape and packaging** — a distinctive bottle shape can be a trade mark (subject to the bar against shapes dictated by function — see registrability).
- **Service marks** — covered by the same definition (services of one person from others).

Related definitions (section 2, by subject): "mark" (device, brand, heading, label, ticket, name, signature, word, letter, numeral, combination of colours, or any combination thereof — as the section provides); "well-known trade mark" (section 2(zk)); "trade name"; "geographical indication" as per the GI Act; "registered proprietor"; "user of a mark" (proprietary use for own goods or by a licensee).

## 3. Absolute grounds for refusal — Section 9

Section 9(1) says a mark shall **not** be registered if, *inter alia*:

- (a) it has **no distinctive character** — i.e. it does not identify the goods as those of a particular person;
- (b) it consists exclusively of signs or indications which may serve, in trade, to **designate the kind, quality, quantity, intended purpose, values, geographical origin or time of production** of the goods or rendering of any service, or other characteristics of the goods/services;
- (c) it consists exclusively of signs or indications that have become **customary in the current language or in the bona fide practices of trade**.

Section 9(2) adds public-interest bars: a mark is not registrable if it is of such a nature as to deceive the public or cause confusion, contains matter likely to hurt religious feelings, is prohibited under the Emblems and Names (Prevention of Improper Use) Act, 1950, or is scandalous/obnoxious (as the sub-section provides).

**Section 9(3) — saving:** despite sub-sections (1) and (2), a mark may be registered where there is **honest concurrent use** or other circumstances, or where, before use in connection with the goods, it has become distinctive of the applicant's goods (acquired distinctiveness / secondary meaning).

### 3.1 Practical application to Ayurvedic names

| Candidate mark | Likely section 9 issue | Path to registration |
|---|---|---|
| "Ayurvedic", "Herbal", "Herbo-mineral", "Rasayana" (for the goods themselves) | s.9(1)(b) — describes kind/characteristics of goods; s.9(1)(a) — no distinctive character | Add a distinctive word/device; or prove acquired distinctiveness under s.9(3) |
| Classical formulation names ("Triphala", "Chyawanprash", "Ashwagandha") | Descriptive of the goods; often customary in trade (s.9(1)(b)-(c)) | Coin a new brand (e.g. "Vaidya Surya Chyawanprash XYZ"); build secondary meaning |
| A surname or personal name (founder's name) | **Not barred by any section.** Section 13 concerns *names of chemical elements and compounds*, not personal names | Registrable if distinctive; if merely the applicant's own name used bona fide, examine s.9(1)(a) and honest concurrent use |
| Geographical names | s.9(1)(b) and/or GI Act (a registered GI cannot be a trade mark) | Only if no GI conflict and the mark has acquired distinctiveness for *these* goods |
| Purely laudatory words ("Gold", "Premium", "Divine") | s.9(1)(a)/(b) | Combine with a distinctive element |

**Common error corrected:** Section 13 of the Trade Marks Act prohibits registration of **names of chemical elements and compounds** (except where in accepted trade usage), **not** surnames or personal names. Personal names are registrable subject to section 9 distinctiveness.

## 4. Relative grounds for refusal — Section 11

Section 11 prevents registration of a mark that is:

1. **identical with an earlier trade mark** and used for identical goods/services; or
2. **similar to an earlier trade mark** and used for identical/similar goods/services, where the use is likely to cause confusion or deception of the public; or
3. **identical or similar to a well-known mark**, where use in India would take unfair advantage of, or be detrimental to, the distinctive character or repute of the well-known mark — **even for dissimilar goods** (the "riding on reputation" / dilution protection in section 11(2)).

Also relevant:

- **Section 11(2) — well-known marks:** protection extends beyond identical/similar goods; the section sets out the considerations (including reputation in India, and the factors used to assess well-known status — read the sub-sections for the exact factor list).
- **Provisions on honest concurrent use and other circumstances** in section 11 (comparable to section 9(3)) permit registration in narrow cases of genuine coexisting use.
- **Section 12-type collision rules:** where two proprietors of nearly identical marks both applied in good faith, the statute provides priority/registration mechanics (read the section; verify numbering).
- **Section 11 also requires the Registrar** to consider marks on the register and to take account of knowledge of a mark in India (as the sub-sections provide).

**Search practice:** always search the Trade Marks Register/Journal for identical and similar marks in the relevant class(es) *and* adjacent classes before filing.

## 5. Other registrability bars

- **Section 13 — Chemical elements/compounds:** no registration of a mark consisting of the name of a chemical element or compound (except where it is in accepted trade usage for the goods). Relevant to mineral/herbo-mineral product naming.
- **Emblems and Names (Prevention of Improper Use) Act, 1950:** bars use of national/state emblems, names of leaders, etc. (cross-referenced by section 9(2)).
- **Shapes dictated by function / necessary to achieve a technical result** and **ornamentality** — examined under the definition and registrability provisions (functionality is a classic refusal ground for packaging shapes).
- **Bad faith** — registration may be refused/invalidated on bad faith grounds (as provided in the Act).
- **Passing off and prior use:** even without registration, an earlier user's common-law rights can defeat a later application (see section 11 "honest concurrent use" and passing off).

## 6. Application, examination and opposition

### 6.1 Procedure (section numbers from the Act's arrangement)

| Step | Section | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Application | 18 | In the prescribed form (Form TM-A under the Trade Marks Rules, 2017); multi-class applications permitted; claim of use or proposed use |
| Examination | 19 | Substantive and formal examination; objections issued (show-cause/Examination Report) |
| Advertisement | 20 | Application accepted and advertised in the Trade Marks Journal |
| **Opposition** | 21 | Any person may give notice of opposition within the prescribed period (four months from advertisement under the Rules — verify current period); the applicant files a counter-statement; evidence and hearing follow |
| Withdrawal of acceptance | 22 | Where acceptance is withdrawn |
| **Registration** | 23 | On acceptance (after refusal/no opposition), the mark is registered and a certificate issued |
| Amendment/division of application | 24 (verify) | Mechanisms for amendment, division of classes |
| **Duration and renewal** | 25 | Registration for **ten years from the date of the application**, renewable for successive periods of **ten years** |
| Removal and restoration | 26 | Removal after non-renewal; restoration mechanism (with fee/surcharge) — verify current time limits |
| Registered and unregistered marks | 27 | Section 27 preserves **passing off** for unregistered marks (subject to the section's limits on restraining infringement of unregistered marks otherwise than by passing off) |

### 6.2 Trade Marks Rules, 2017 — practical forms

- **Form TM-A** — application for registration (fresh/divisional/series/multi-class).
- **Form TM-O** — notice of opposition (within four months of advertisement — verify).
- **Form TM-M** — miscellaneous matters (powers of attorney, amendments).
- **Form TM-R / TM-U and associated forms** — renewal, restoration, removal and correction (verify current form numbers before advising; forms are amended from time to time).
- Fees are in the First Schedule to the Rules (differ for individuals/startups/small entities vs others; e-filing concessions exist — verify current amounts).

## 7. Duration, renewal and removal (Sections 25 and 26)

1. **Term:** ten years **from the date of application** (not from the date of certificate).
2. **Renewal:** application for renewal must be filed within the prescribed window before expiry; renewal is for ten years at a time; there is a **surcharge** window for late renewal and a **restoration** mechanism after removal (verify the current limits — commonly six months before/after expiry and a restoration period under section 26).
3. **Non-use vulnerability:** a registered mark can be removed for **non-use** (three years' continuous non-use is the statutory trigger period commonly applied under the removal provisions — verify the section and the three/five year references before quoting).
4. **Use evidence:** maintain dated invoices, advertisements, packaging versions and market share data — use evidence is essential both to defend against removal and to prove acquired distinctiveness.

## 8. Assignment and licensing

### 8.1 Assignment and transmission

- Chapter V of the Act governs **assignment and transmission** of trade marks (sections **37 and following**, including assignment **with the goodwill** of the business, and assignment **without goodwill** in respect of proposed use — read sections 37-40 for the mechanics and restrictions).
- Assignment must be in writing and signed by the parties; particulars are recorded in the register.
- Restrictions apply where an assignment would cause **deception or confusion** (the Act bars assignments likely to deceive — read the relevant section before advising on brand transfers).

### 8.2 Licensing / registered user

- Licensing is by agreement; the Act provides for a licensee to be recorded as a **registered user** so the licence is effective against third parties (provisions on licensed use and registration of the registered user — in the Chapter dealing with use of trade marks; commonly cited around sections 48-49 — **verify the section numbers in the official text**).
- A quality-control clause is essential: uncontrolled ("naked") licensing can destroy the mark's distinctiveness and lead to loss/invalidity.
- For Ayurveda franchise/manufacturing ("loan licence" manufacturing) arrangements, the trade-mark licence and the drug-manufacturing authorisation must be aligned.

### 8.3 Collective and certification marks

The Act also provides for registration of **collective marks** (associations of producers/traders) and **certification marks** (marks certifying origin, material, mode of manufacture or quality) — useful for AYUSH quality-seal schemes (verify the sections creating these regimes).

## 9. Infringement — Section 29

**Section 29 — Infringement of registered trade marks:** a registered mark is infringed by a person who, not being a registered proprietor or a permitted user, uses in the course of trade a mark which is **identical with or deceptively similar to** the registered mark, for identical/similar goods or services, in a manner likely to cause confusion, deception or mistake.

Key sub-sections (by subject):

- Use as a **trade name** or part of a business name, or in advertising, can constitute infringement (section 29(4)-type cases — verify numbering).
- Use **otherwise than as a mark** (e.g. descriptive/nominative use) is addressed by the section's own limits.
- **Section 30 — Acts not amounting to infringement:** honest use of one's own name, description of goods, good-faith use of another's mark to indicate the nature/quality/connection of goods (nominative/descriptive fair use), and other statutory exceptions. Read section 30 before alleging infringement.
- **Passing off (section 27 read with common law):** for unregistered marks, the action is passing off — requiring goodwill, misrepresentation and damage (the classic *Reckitt & Colman* elements as applied by Indian courts).

## 10. Well-known marks (Section 11(2) and Section 2(zk))

1. **Definition:** section 2(zk) defines "well-known trade mark" as a mark which has become so to the substantial segment of the public which uses such goods or receives such services, that the use of such mark in relation to other goods/services would be likely to be taken as indicating a connection in the course of trade (as the section provides).
2. **Protection:** section 11(2) — registration is refused, and infringement can be found, for marks identical/similar to a well-known mark used for dissimilar goods where the use takes unfair advantage of or is detrimental to the mark.
3. **Assessment:** the section sets out the relevant considerations (knowledge of the mark in India, extent of registration/use, enforcement record, etc. — read the sub-sections).
4. **Record:** the Registrar maintains a record of well-known marks (as provided in the Act) — verify the mechanism before advising.

**Ayurveda angle:** foreign AYUSH/wellness brands entering India, and Indian brands expanding abroad, should assess well-known status evidence early; conversely, Indian heritage brands should file early to build Indian reputation evidence.

| **Jurisdiction** | Section 134 | No suit for infringement of a registered trade mark shall be instituted in any court **inferior to a District Court** |

**Criminal offences:** the Act also creates offences for **falsifying trade marks** and **selling goods bearing falsely applied marks** (Chapter on offences; commonly cited around sections 103-106 — **verify the section numbers and penalty amounts** before quoting; note that penalty provisions have been amended by reform legislation).

## 12. Relevance to Ayurvedic products and to other IP rights

1. **Naming strategy:** coin a distinctive brand and avoid pure descriptive/classical names; classical formulation names are usually descriptive/customary for the goods (section 9(1)(b)-(c)).
2. **Class coverage:** Ayurvedic medicines are typically class 5; foods/supplements class 30/29/32 (Ayurveda Aahar); cosmetics class 3; wellness services class 44 — file in all classes actually used or planned.
3. **Coexistence with GI:** a registered GI cannot be registered as a trade mark for the same goods (GI Act section 25); a place name for a GI-goods product needs care.
4. **Coexistence with patents/designs:** the brand (TM), the bottle shape (design), the label artwork (copyright), the formulation (patent only if it clears section 3(p)) and the know-how (trade secret) are separate rights — manage them together.
5. **Domain names and social handles:** trade mark rights support takedown of confusingly similar domains/handles (via the INDRP/UDRP and platform policies).
6. **Advertising overlap:** a registered mark still cannot be used to make claims barred by the Drugs and Magic Remedies Act — registration does not cure a misleading claim.

## 13. What an Ayurvedic product owner must check

- [ ] Clear the proposed name/logo by searching the Trade Marks Register and Journal (identical + similar marks, relevant and adjacent classes).
- [ ] Assess registrability under **section 9** (distinctive? descriptive? customary?) and **section 11** (earlier marks? well-known marks?) before filing.
- [ ] Decide filing basis: "proposed use" vs actual use; file **before** launch; keep use evidence from day one.
- [ ] Diary the opposition window (four months from advertisement — verify) and be ready to file **Form TM-O** or a counter-statement.
- [ ] Diary the **ten-year renewal** (from filing date) and the restoration window under sections 25-26.
- [ ] Record licences as **registered users** and include quality control; keep manufacturer/franchise aligned with the drug licence.
- [ ] For enforcement, confirm the mark is **registered** (section 29) or build a **passing off** case (section 27) with goodwill evidence; file in a **District Court or higher** (section 134).
- [ ] Preserve evidence of use (invoices, ads, sales figures) — needed for renewal, non-use defence and damages.
- [ ] Consider well-known mark evidence if the brand is famous (section 11(2), section 2(zk)).
- [ ] Never assume registration legalises a deceptive or prohibited claim (sections 9(2), plus the Drugs and Magic Remedies Act and consumer law).
