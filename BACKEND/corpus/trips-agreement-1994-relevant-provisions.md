# Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS Agreement, 1994) — Relevant Provisions

## 1. What this instrument is

The Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights ("TRIPS
Agreement", sometimes referred to by the French acronym **ADPIC**) was negotiated
during the Uruguay Round of multilateral trade negotiations and signed at
**Marrakesh on 15 April 1994**. It entered into force on **1 January 1995** as an
Annex 1C agreement to the Agreement Establishing the World Trade Organization.
Every Member of the WTO is bound by it, including India (a founding Member of the
WTO on 1 January 1995).

TRIPS is a **minimum-standards** agreement: it does not create a single global
intellectual property right. It requires each Member to provide a baseline of
protection and enforcement in its own national law, by specified deadlines, and
it allows Members to go further ("flexibility to go beyond the minimum").

### Structure of the Agreement

| Part | Subject | Articles (subject) |
|---|---|---|
| Part I | General provisions and basic principles | Arts. 1–7 (scope, national treatment, MFN, objectives) |
| Part II | Standards concerning the availability, scope and use of IP | Arts. 8–40 |
| Part III | Enforcement of IP rights (civil, administrative, provisional, border, criminal) | Arts. 41–61 |
| Part IV | Acquisition and maintenance of IP; co-operation | Arts. 62–65 |
| Part V | Dispute settlement and transitional arrangements | Arts. 63–65 |
| Part VI | Institutional arrangements; technical assistance | Arts. 66–67 |
| Part VII | Final provisions | Arts. 66–73 |

## 2. Objectives and principles — the flexibility clauses

### 2.1 Article 7 — Objectives

Article 7 states that the protection and enforcement of intellectual property
rights should contribute to the **promotion of technological innovation** and to
the **transfer and dissemination of technology**, to the mutual advantage of
producers and users of technological knowledge **and in a manner conducive to
social and economic welfare**, and to a balance of rights and obligations.

### 2.2 Article 8 — Principles

Article 8 has three paragraphs and is the most frequently invoked "flexibility"
provision of the Agreement:

- **Article 8(1)** — Members shall, in formulating or amending their laws and
  regulations, give **due consideration to the need to protect public health and
  nutrition, and to promote the public interest in sectors of vital importance to
  their socio-economic and technological development**.
- **Article 8(2)** — Members **may** need to adopt appropriate measures to
  **prevent the abuse of intellectual property rights by right holders** or the
  resort to practices which unreasonably restrain trade or adversely affect the
  international transfer of technology.
- **Article 8(3)** — Members shall, **when adopting measures implementing this
  Agreement, be free to adopt appropriate measures provided that they are
  consistent with the provisions of the Agreement**.

### 2.3 Why Articles 7 and 8 matter for traditional knowledge

Together with Article 63 (transparency) and the interpretation rules in the WTO
Agreement, Articles 7 and 8 are the clauses relied on by commentators, the WTO
Secretariat and developing-country Members for the proposition that TRIPS is
*not* a prohibition on protecting traditional knowledge (TK), associated
knowledge and genetic resources — provided any such measure is consistent with
the rest of the Agreement. In the French authentic text (ADPIC), the reference to
the freedom to adopt appropriate measures is Article 8(3), which is why TK
literature sometimes cites "ADPIC, article 8(3)" alongside "TRIPS, Article 8(1)".

**Reading for a TK-using product owner:** Articles 7 and 8 support the domestic
use of disclosure obligations, prior-informed-consent conditions and benefit-
sharing requirements (for example India's Biological Diversity Act, 2002), and
they support reading TRIPS provisions on patents and exceptions in a manner that
preserves public-health and developmental policy space.

## 3. Basic principles: national treatment and most-favoured-nation treatment

- **Article 4 (national treatment)** — no discrimination between foreign and
  own-nationals in respect of the protection of intellectual property (subject to
  the existing multilateral agreements listed in Article 4, including the Paris
  and Berne Conventions and the Rome Convention).
- **Article 5 (most-favoured-nation treatment)** — any advantage, favour,
  privilege or immunity granted by a Member to nationals of any other country
  shall be accorded immediately and unconditionally to the nationals of all other
  Members.
- **Article 6 (exhaustion)** — TRIPS leaves exhaustion (first sale / international
  exhaustion) to national law: for the purposes of dispute settlement, and
  subject to Articles 3 and 4, nothing in the Agreement may be used to address the
  question of exhaustion.

## 4. Copyright and related rights (Articles 9–14)

| TRIPS article | Subject | Plain-language effect |
|---|---|---|
| Art. 9 | Berne Convention obligation | Members must comply with Articles 1–21 of the Berne Convention (1971) but **not** moral rights (Art. 6bis Berne). |
| Art. 10 | Computer programs and compilations | Computer programs are protected as literary works; compilations/data selections that are original by reason of selection or arrangement are protected but do not extend to the data itself. |
| Art. 11 | Rental rights | Producers of phonograms and makers of films have a right to authorise or prohibit commercial rental to the public; computer-program rental is addressed separately. |
| Art. 12 | Term of protection | Not less than **50 years** from the end of the calendar year of authorship/death of the author. |
| Art. 13 | Phonograms | Not less than **50 years** from the end of the year of fixation or publication. |
| Art. 14 | Performers, producers of phonograms, broadcasting organisations | Minimum rights and terms of not less than **20 years** for the relevant acts. |

**India's implementation:** the Copyright Act, 1957 (subject matter in Section 13,
rights of authors in Section 14, term of copyright in Section 22 — life of the
author plus 60 years for most works, moral rights in Section 57). Note that
India's term is **longer** than the TRIPS minimum, which is permitted.

**Ayurvedic relevance:** copyright protects the *expression* in a formulation
book, a proprietary pharmacopoeia, a website, a course, artwork and labelling
copy — never the underlying recipe, process or fact. Copyright arises
automatically; registration is evidentiary only.

## 5. Trade marks (Articles 15–21)

| TRIPS article | Subject |
|---|---|
| Art. 15 | Signs (including words, personal names, designs, letters, numerals, colours, combinations) must be capable of distinguishing goods/services; protection may be made dependent on distinctiveness acquired through use; **descriptive** signs may be excluded from registration/invalidated where they lack distinctiveness; Members may require use as a filing condition. |
| Art. 16 | "Likelihood of confusion" is the core infringement standard for identical or similar marks/goods. |
| Art. 17 | Exceptions (e.g., fair use of descriptive terms) are permitted. |
| Art. 18 | Initial registration and each renewal shall be for a term of **at least 7 years**; renewal shall be *indefinitely* for successive periods. |
| Art. 19 | Where use is required to maintain a right, the right may be cancelled only after **3 years' non-use**, and where the holder shows valid reasons for non-use (obstacles such as import restrictions). |
| Art. 20 | Use in the form as registered must be a condition of maintaining the right, but actual use must not be required in a form that would be an obstacle to registration. |
| Art. 21 | Assignment and related matters (including the relationship between assignment and the goodwill of the business) — see the official text for the exact wording. |

**India's implementation:** the Trade Marks Act, 1999 — absolute grounds for
refusal (Section 9, including descriptiveness and laudatory terms), relative
grounds (Section 11), use before registration (Section 12), duration of
registration 10 years renewable (Section 25), removal for non-use after 3 years
(Section 47), and the passing-off action (Section 27(2)) for unregistered marks.

**Ayurvedic relevance:** a brand such as an Ashwagandha or Chyawanprash house
mark is protectable; a mark that is *purely descriptive* of the herb, the
Ayurvedic tradition or the therapeutic effect will be refused or invalidated.
Formulation names and "classical" names are generally weak — build brand
distinctiveness with a coined or arbitrary element.

## 6. Geographical indications (Articles 22–24)

- **Article 22 (definition and basic protection)** — a geographical indication
  identifies goods as originating in a territory, region or locality where a
  given quality, reputation or other characteristic is essentially attributable
  to that geographical origin. Members shall provide the legal means to prevent
  (a) any use of a GI that **misleads the public** as to the geographical origin,
  and (b) any use that constitutes **unfair competition**. GI protection does not
  require proof of goodwill or confusion. **Article 22.4** allows the protection
  of a GI that, although literally true, falsely represents to the public that
  the goods originate in another territory.
- **Article 23 (higher protection for wines and spirits)** — for wines and
  spirits, protection shall be provided even where the true origin is indicated
  or the indication is used in translation or accompanied by expressions such as
  "type", "kind", "style", "imitation" or the like. **Article 23.4** requires
  Members, on request, to refuse or invalidate the registration of a trademark
  that consists of or contains such a GI for wines or spirits not possessing that
  origin.
- **Article 24 (international negotiations; exceptions and transitions)** —
  contains the negotiation mandate (built into later WTO work on GIs) and
  transitional/grandfathering rules, including protection for trademarks applied
  for or registered in good faith before the date of application of the Agreement,
  and a transition period for certain wines/spirits GIs already in use before
  15 April 1994 (commonly described as a 10-year period). **Verify the exact
  paragraph numbers and dates against the official text.**

**India's implementation:** the Geographical Indications of Goods (Registration
and Protection) Act, 1999, with the GI Registry at Chennai. India's first GI
registration was **Darjeeling Tea** (2000), followed by a growing register
including Basmati, Tirupathi Laddu, Nilgiri tea and others.

**Ayurvedic relevance:** a regional Ayurvedic raw drug (for example a
large-scale cultivated or wild-harvested drug with a recognised place of origin)
can be protected as a GI, but the GI belongs to the *community of producers*
collectively, not to a single manufacturer — a company can only use it under
conformity rules.

## 7. Industrial designs (Articles 25–26)

- **Article 25 (requirements for protection)** — Members shall provide for the
  protection of independently created designs that are **new or original**;
  Members may provide that protection is not extended to designs dictated by
  technical or functional requirements. Members shall provide the possibility
  that protection be available for **textile designs** (the article contains a
  minimum period reference for textile designs — verify the figure in the
  official text; the commonly cited minimum is at least 10 years for textiles).
- **Article 26 (rights, exceptions, term)** — subject matter, the exclusive
  right to prevent third parties from making, selling or importing articles
  bearing the design, **exceptions** analogous to those permitted for patents
  (Article 30), non-voluntary licences by reference to Article 31, and a term of
  protection of **not less than 10 years** from the filing date (renewal
  permitted for further periods).

**India's implementation:** the Designs Act, 2000 — registration for an initial
term of **10 years** from the date of application, extendable by **5 years** on
application (total 15 years).

**Ayurvedic relevance:** the *shape and configuration* of an Ayurveda product
bottle, packaging, dispensing device or dosage form, and the graphical/ornamental
layout of a kit, are protectable as designs; the formulation inside is not.

## 8. Patents — minimum standards (Articles 27–29 and beyond)

### 8.1 Article 27 — Patentable subject matter

- **Article 27(1)** — patents shall be available for **inventions in all fields
  of technology**, provided they are **novel**, involve an **inventive step**
  (non-obviousness) and are **capable of industrial application**; patents shall
  be available **without discrimination** as to whether products are imported or
  locally produced, and as to the field of technology.
- **Article 27(2)** — exclusions from patentability may be made to protect
  *ordre public* or morality, including to protect human, animal or plant life
  or health or to avoid serious prejudice to the environment, provided such
  exclusion is not made merely because exploitation is prohibited by domestic law.
- **Article 27(3)(a)** — Members **may** exclude from patentability
  **diagnostic, therapeutic and surgical methods for the treatment of humans or
  animals**. This is the provision under which classical Ayurvedic therapeutic
  *methods* are generally outside patentability.
- **Article 27(3)(b)** — Members **may** exclude from patentability **plants,
  animals and essentially biological processes** for the production of plants or
  animals other than non-biological and microbiological processes. However,
  Members shall provide for the **protection of plant varieties either by patents
  or by an effective *sui generis* system or by any combination thereof**.
  - **Microorganisms and microbiological processes are therefore patentable**
    (they are expressly carved out of the exclusion).
  - India's response: Section 3(j) of the Patents Act, 1970 excludes plants and
    parts thereof (including seeds, varieties and species, and essentially
    biological processes for the production and propagation of plants), while
    plant varieties are protected under the Protection of Plant Varieties and
    Farmers' Rights Act, 2001 — an "effective sui generis system".

### 8.2 Article 28 — Rights conferred

A patent confers the right to **prevent third parties from making, using,
offering for sale, selling or importing** the patented product, and (for process
patents) from using the process and using, offering for sale, selling or
importing the product obtained **directly** by that process.

### 8.3 Article 29 — Disclosure by the applicant

- **Article 29(1)** — the applicant shall disclose the invention in a manner
  **sufficiently clear and complete** for it to be carried out by a person skilled
  in the art, and may be required to indicate the **best mode** contemplated for
  carrying out the invention (the Agreement leaves the best-mode requirement to
  national law rather than mandating it).
- **Article 29(2)** — Members may require the applicant to furnish information
  concerning **corresponding foreign applications and grants**.

**Ayurvedic relevance:** a bio-prospecting invention (for example a
fermentation-derived metabolite from an Ayurvedic herb, or a defined microbial
consortium used in preparation) must be disclosed so that a skilled person can
reproduce it. Where the invention uses a microorganism that cannot be described
in writing alone, the **Budapest Treaty deposit** supplies the missing part of
the disclosure (see the Budapest Treaty document in this corpus).

### 8.4 Article 30 — Exceptions to rights

Members may provide **limited exceptions** to the exclusive rights conferred by
a patent, provided such exceptions do not unreasonably conflict with a normal
exploitation of the patent and do not unreasonably prejudice the legitimate
interests of the patent owner, taking account of the legitimate interests of
third parties (the "three-step test").

**India's implementation:** Section 30 (Limitation of rights) and the Bolar-type
research/supply exemption in Section 107A of the Patents Act, 1970, read with
the regulatory provisions of the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940.

### 8.5 Articles 31 and 31bis — Compulsory licensing and government use

**Article 31 ("other use without the authorisation of the right holder")**
permits a Member to allow use of a patented invention (including government use)
without the patent owner's consent, subject to cumulative conditions, including:

1. **case-by-case** authorisation;
2. prior efforts to obtain a licence on **reasonable commercial terms** (may be
   waived in a **national emergency, extreme urgency, or a case of public
   non-commercial use**);
3. the authorisation shall be **predominantly for the domestic market**;
4. the scope and duration shall be **limited to the purpose** for which it was
   authorised;
5. **adequate remuneration** paid to the right holder, taking into account the
   economic value of the authorisation;
6. the authorisation shall be **non-exclusive and non-assignable**;
7. authorisation for a product imported for export (the original Article 31
   "domestic market" rule) is dealt with by Article 31bis.

**Article 31bis and the Annex** (added by the WTO in December 2005) create the
mechanism allowing a compulsory licence to authorise the **export of
pharmaceuticals to countries with insufficient or no manufacturing capacity**.

**The Doha Declaration on the TRIPS Agreement and Public Health**
(WT/MIN(01)/DEC/2, adopted 14 November 2001) confirms that TRIPS "can and should
be interpreted and implemented in a manner supportive of WTO Members' right to
protect public health and, in particular, to promote access to medicines for
all", and that nothing in TRIPS prevents Members from taking measures to protect
public health.

**India's implementation:** Sections 84 (compulsory licence on
non-working/anti-competitive conduct), 92 (national emergency, extreme urgency
or public non-commercial use) and 92A (export to countries with insufficient
manufacturing capacity) of the Patents Act, 1970, with the Compulsory Licence
Rules. India's best-known decision is the 2013 compulsory licence for the cancer
drug sorafenib (Natco v. Bayer) — see the Indian case-law compendium in this
corpus.

### 8.6 Article 32 — Revocation / Article 33 — Term / Article 34 — Process patents

- **Term:** patents shall be available for a term of **not less than 20 years
  from the filing date** (Article 33).
- **Article 32** provides an opportunity for review/revocation of the decision
  granting the patent.
- **Article 34** addresses **process patents for products** in infringement
  proceedings: where the subject matter of a patent is a product obtained by a
  process, the judicial authorities may order the defendant to prove that the
  process used to obtain the identical product is **not** the patented process
  (reversal of the burden of proof, subject to protection of the defendant's
  legitimate trade secrets).

**India's implementation:** term of 20 years from the date of filing
(Section 53), revocation grounds (Section 64), and the process-patent burden
reversal in Section 104 read with Section 107 of the Patents Act, 1970.

## 9. Undisclosed information and test data (Article 39)

- **Article 39.1** — as a condition of protecting undisclosed data submitted to
  governments or governmental agencies, Members shall treat them as confidential.
- **Article 39.2** — **trade secrets / undisclosed information** must be
  protected if the information is secret, has commercial value because it is
  secret, and has been subject to **reasonable steps to keep it secret**.
- **Article 39.3** — for **pharmaceutical products involving new chemical
  entities**, undisclosed test or other data submitted for marketing approval
  shall be protected against unfair commercial use.

**Ayurvedic relevance:** proprietary extraction parameters, standardisation
methods, clinical datasets, supplier know-how and TK held under access-and-
benefit-sharing terms are protectable as trade secrets where reasonable secrecy
measures are taken — but only for as long as they stay secret. In India the
analogous protection sits in Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 and in
contract, not in the IP statutes.

## 10. Enforcement (Articles 41–61)

| Section of Part III | Articles | Content |
|---|---|---|
| General principles | Art. 41 | Enforcement procedures must be **fair and equitable**, not unnecessarily **costly or complex**, without **unreasonable time limits** or unwarranted delays; decisions shall preferably be in writing and reasons given. |
| Civil and administrative procedures | Arts. 42–49 | Right of information, evidence, provisional and **preliminary injunctions**, damages (including adequate compensation and, where appropriate, expenses), right to order disposal of infringing goods. |
| Provisional measures | Art. 50 | Injunctions *ante videlem* — judicial authorities may order prompt and effective provisional measures to prevent infringement and preserve evidence. |
| Border measures | Arts. 51–60 | Application by right holders to customs authorities to suspend the release of goods suspected of infringing marks or copyright. |
| Criminal procedures | Art. 61 | **Wilful** trademark counterfeiting and copyright piracy on a **commercial scale** shall be subject to criminal penalties including imprisonment and/or fines. |

**India's implementation:** the Code of Civil Procedure (Order XXXIX
injunctions), the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, the Copyright Act, 1957
(Sections 64, 64A, 65), the Trade Marks Act, 1999 (infringement/passing-off
actions and criminal provisions), and the customs framework for detention of
infringing goods.

## 11. Transparency, dispute settlement and transitional provisions

- **Article 63 (transparency)** — laws, regulations, final judicial decisions and
  administrative rulings made public; notification to the TRIPS Council.
- **Article 64 (dispute settlement)** — the Dispute Settlement Understanding
  (DSU) applies. In practice this means TRIPS disputes are brought by
  *governments* (for example the India–EU and Brazil–EU "asbestos-style"
  pharmaceutical/GI disputes), not by private right holders.
- **Articles 65–66 (transitional arrangements)** — longer transition periods for
  least-developed-country Members (Article 66.1) and a technology-transfer
  undertaking (Article 66.2).
- **Article 67 (technical co-operation)** — developed-country Members shall
  provide technical and financial co-operation.

## 13. What this means for an Ayurvedic product owner

1. **You cannot patent the classical knowledge itself.** A known Ayurvedic
   formulation or a known use is not novel or inventive (Article 27(1)); classical
   therapeutic methods are excluded (Article 27(3)(a)) and Indian Sections 3(a),
   3(d), 3(e) and 3(h) go further. What *is* patentable: a genuinely new,
   non-obvious and industrially applicable technical solution — a novel
   extraction/purification step, a defined composition with unexpected and
   demonstrated *enhanced* efficacy, a delivery system, a fermentation process, a
   standardisation method, a device.
2. **Disclosure discipline.** If the invention uses a microorganism, deposit it
   under the Budapest Treaty and file the receipt with the complete specification
   (Article 29(1)).
3. **Plant material needs a *sui generis* route.** New plant varieties go to the
   PPV&FR Act, 2001, not to the patent (Article 27(3)(b)).
4. **Flexibility is lawful.** Compulsory licensing, research exemptions and TK/
   benefit-sharing conditions are TRIPS-consistent when applied under Articles 7,
   8, 30 and 31 (and, for public health, the Doha Declaration).
5. **Enforcement is a government-and-court system.** Expect civil injunctions,
   damages and customs action; criminal exposure applies to wilful
   counterfeiting on a commercial scale (Articles 41–61).
6. **Geographical indication is a collective tool**, useful for a regional
   Ayurvedic raw drug brand, but it does not give one company exclusive use
   (Article 22).
7. **Know your obligations abroad.** Every market you export to applies its own
   TRIPS-plus rules; check the local statute rather than assuming the Indian
   position travels with the product.
